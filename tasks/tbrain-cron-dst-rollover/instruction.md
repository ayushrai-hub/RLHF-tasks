The Go project in `/app` builds `dstcron`, a small utility that answers a single question for a daily cron-style job: given a local time zone and a wall-clock fire time, when does the job next run, expressed as a UTC instant? It is meant for hosts whose local clock shifts twice a year, and it is deliberately self-contained: the time zone is described entirely in the input file, so no system time zone database is consulted and the answer is fully determined by the file.

The program is run as `dstcron <inputfile>`. The input uses one directive per line. `offset_std <minutes>` and `offset_dst <minutes>` give the local clock's offset east of UTC during standard time and during daylight time, respectively, each as a whole number of minutes. `spring_forward <utcsecond>` gives the UTC second at which the clock jumps forward from the standard offset to the daylight offset, skipping a stretch of wall-clock readings that never occur that day. `fall_back <utcsecond>` gives the UTC second at which the clock falls back from the daylight offset to the standard offset, repeating a stretch of wall-clock readings that occur twice that day. The daylight offset is the one in force from the spring instant up to, but not including, the fall instant; the standard offset is in force at every other instant. `fire_at <HH:MM>` gives the daily schedule as a local wall-clock time of day. Each `next <utcsecond>` line is a query: a UTC instant from which to look forward. For every query, in the order the queries appear, the tool prints on its own line the UTC second of the next moment, strictly after the query instant, at which the local wall clock reads the scheduled time of day. Output is those numbers and nothing else.

Local time is UTC plus whichever offset is in force at that UTC instant, and the next fire is the earliest qualifying instant after the query. Two situations need care, and the contract for each is fixed. When the scheduled wall-clock time lands inside the stretch the clock skips on the spring-forward day, that reading never actually occurs, and the job must fire at the instant the clock advances. When the scheduled wall-clock time lands inside the stretch the clock repeats on the fall-back day, that reading occurs twice, and the job must fire at the first (earlier) of the two occurrences. On every ordinary day, and for any query whose next fire is far from either transition, the answer is simply the scheduled wall-clock time carried back to the UTC instant that produces it under the offset actually in force there.

Operators report that the answers are off. Even on an ordinary day, well away from any transition, the printed UTC instant is shifted from the correct one by exactly the size of the time zone's offset: the conversion between the local wall-clock target and the UTC instant is being applied in the wrong direction, so every ordinary fire comes out displaced by one offset width. That displacement is the most visible symptom, but operators have also noticed that the results around the days the clock changes do not line up with the documented behavior for a skipped or a repeated wall-clock time.

Make `dstcron` print the correct next-fire UTC instant for every query: ordinary days must land exactly on the scheduled wall-clock time under the offset truly in force, and the days the clock changes must honor the documented rules for a wall-clock time that is skipped and for one that occurs twice. The build is the standard Go toolchain (`go build ./...` from `/app`).
