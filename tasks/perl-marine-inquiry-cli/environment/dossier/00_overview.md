# The Loss of the SS Marisol: Case Overview

This dossier collects every paper, deposition, log entry, and private letter relevant to the loss of the steamship Marisol, which foundered off the Carrack Shoal in fog on the night of 14 November 1924, on a voyage from Liverpool to Lisbon. It has been assembled from the file of the Board of Trade's preliminary inquiry, from the records of the underwriters at whose request a fuller investigation was commissioned, from the depositions taken from the survivors, and from the working notes of the assessor (you) appointed to sift the matter to the bottom and report a finding as to the true cause of the loss.

You should read it as a working folder, not as a finished narrative. The papers are out of order in places. The voices do not always agree. Some entries contradict others. That is the nature of a marine casualty in which the witnesses are drawn from a single ship's company, in which the owner has an interest in one answer and the crew in another, and in which the most important men either went down with the ship or have not been seen since she struck.

## What you already know

The Marisol was an ageing single-screw cargo steamer of 2,840 tons gross, built 1898, owned and managed by Mr Marcus Veil of the firm of Veil and Penhallow, ship managers and agents, of Liverpool. She cleared the Mersey on the morning tide of 12 November 1924, bound for Lisbon, with a declared general cargo the principal item of which was a consignment of machine tools said to be of considerable value. She carried a crew of twenty-six and no passengers.

On the night of 14 November, in thick fog off the Portuguese approaches, she stranded on the Carrack Shoal. Within a remarkably short time of striking she was making water faster than her pumps could hold, and her master, Captain Aldous Frane, gave the order to abandon. Two of her three boats got away. Twenty-one men, including the master, were taken up the following forenoon by a coasting steamer and landed at Oporto. Five men were lost, among them the chief engineer's relief and, it is presumed, the second engineer, Edvard Lund, who was last seen going below and whose body was never recovered. The Marisol herself slipped off the shoal on the falling tide and went down in deep water, where she now lies.

The Board of Trade convened a preliminary inquiry at Liverpool in December 1924. Its provisional view, set out in the papers that follow, is that the Marisol was lost by stranding in fog, an honest marine accident, and that such blame as attaches anywhere attaches to the navigation of the ship, that is to say, to her master. The underwriters, who stand to pay a very large sum upon the hull and a larger sum upon the cargo, were not satisfied with so tidy an answer, and it is at their instance that this fuller inquiry has been opened and that you have been appointed to it.

You will read all of the relevant statements in the pages that follow.

## The persons concerned

Seven persons recur throughout this file, and you should fix their names and parts in your mind before you read further. Their identifiers, given in the parties section, are the form in which you will record any finding against them.

Captain Aldous Frane, master of the Marisol, who brought her in upon the shoal and gave the order to abandon, and against whom the rumour of negligence has been raised. Mr Marcus Veil, managing owner, who was ashore in Liverpool throughout and never set foot aboard for the voyage. Mr Edvard Lund, second engineer, lost in the casualty, last seen going below as she filled. Mr Daniel Okonkwo, first mate, who had the deck for part of the run-in and who was taken up in the master's boat. Mr Tomas Such, chief engineer, who was off watch and asleep in his bunk when she struck. Miss Hester Cardew, the independent cargo surveyor who tallied the lading at Liverpool on behalf of a sub-underwriter. And Mr Mercer, the underwriter's agent who placed the policies and who holds the schedules.

You will read their depositions, their letters, and the records that bear upon each of them.

## The character of the inquiry

This is an assessment, not a transcription. The dossier records the Board of Trade's working theory and the impressions of the witnesses alongside the hard records, but those theories are not your finding, and you are not bound by them. Your task is to determine, from the whole body of the evidence and not from the surface of it, the true cause of the loss of the Marisol: by what means she came to founder, and at whose hand the thing was done, if it was done by a hand at all.

The cause that the file appears to favour, and the cause that a hasty reading will yield, is an honest stranding in fog. You are asked to be slower than that. The records in this dossier do not all point the same way, and the few that point the true way do not announce themselves. They lie among the others, plainly stated but unflagged, and they will only join into a conclusion if you are prepared to do the work of joining them.

## What the dossier contains

The dossier is laid out as nine numbered files. The particulars of the vessel and the voyage come first after this overview, because you cannot follow any deposition without a clear picture of the ship, her holds, her machinery, and the track she ran. The procedure of the inquiry, and the form in which a finding is to be entered, come next. Then the parties, the full depositions, the owner's correspondence and private papers, the reconstructed timeline of the loss as the inquiry has pieced it together, the records log, the rules for entering the finding, and finally the appendices, which carry the salvage report, the policy schedules, and the surveyor's tallies.

Read them in order on a first pass. After that, return to whichever section the day's reading throws new light upon. The depositions in particular reward a second and a third reading. Men say a great deal more than they intend, and they contradict one another in ways that show only when you read three statements back to back.

## A note on what the dossier will not tell you

The dossier will not tell you, in plain words, why the Marisol was lost. That conclusion it leaves to you. But every fact you need to reach it is somewhere in these pages, set down without ornament and without emphasis, among a great many facts that you will need to set aside. Some of the records explain away the very things that matter. Some omit to connect facts that, connected, would settle the case in an afternoon.

If at any point you feel the answer is obvious, be suspicious of yourself. A ship lost in fog is the easiest thing in the world to file and forget, and there are men with an interest in your filing it so. Hold your conclusion lightly. Read the salvage diver's report and ask yourself whether a ship that merely took the ground would fill as this one filled. Read the surveyor's tally against the manifest and ask yourself what was actually in the holds. Read the policy schedule and ask yourself what the hull and her cargo were truly worth, and what they were insured for. Read the owner's letters and ask yourself what was being arranged ashore before ever she sailed.

Then read them all again.

## How this dossier is arranged, and how to read it

The dossier is laid out so that a hasty reader will reach the comfortable answer and a careful one the true answer. That is not an accident of arrangement; it is the shape the materials themselves impose, for the loss was contrived to look like an accident, and the file inherits the contrivance. The preliminary report, the bulk of the depositions, and the rumour all lie on the surface and all point one way. The records that undo them lie deeper, in the appendices and the records log, and point another way, and no one of them, taken alone, is enough; the case is in their conjunction.

You should therefore not expect the dossier to hand you its conclusion. It will hand you, plainly and without emphasis, every fact you need, scattered among a great many facts you do not. The surface of the file will press an answer upon you with some confidence. Take nothing from the surface that the records do not bear. When the surface and the records disagree, the records govern; that is the first rule of this inquiry and very nearly the whole of it.

Read the files in order on a first pass. Draw and examine the records. Read the depositions twice. Then return to the records log and the appendices and read the five decisive records together, one against another, and ask what single account of the night is consistent with all of them at once. There is one such account, and only one, and it is not the account the file first presses upon you. When you have it, and have tested it against every record and found it holds where the comfortable account fails, enter your finding, and not before.

Read carefully. Reason slowly. Enter your finding only when the evidence, and not the file, has made it sure.
