# How the Finding is Entered

This section sets out the procedure under which the assessor's finding is entered to the case office, and the form it must take. Read it carefully before you enter anything. A finding entered in the wrong form, or before its preconditions are met, will be refused, and the office will not entertain a second.

## The form of the finding

The finding is entered as a single formal statement to the case office, settling the cause of the loss of the Marisol. It must state four particulars, all required.

The party responsible. The identifier of the party found responsible for the loss, drawn from the parties (file 03), in the form par-something. The party named must be the party whom the whole of the evidence makes responsible for the loss, not the party the file appears at first to favour.

The means of the loss. The office's short reference for the mechanism by which the ship was lost. Where the finding is that the ship was deliberately sunk by opening the sea to her low down in the engine room, the means is entered as the scuttling of the ship by the open sea-cock, reference scuttle-seacock. (Were the finding an honest stranding, the means would be entered as stranding in fog; that is the means the preliminary inquiry would have entered, and the assessor should enter it only if the evidence supports it, which the records in file 07 do not.)

The place of the loss. The office's reference for the place aboard where the act that lost the ship was done. Where the ship was lost by the opening of the sea-cock and the disabling of the bilge valves, the place is the engine room, reference loc-engine-room, that being where the main sea inlet and the bilge manifold are and where the act was done. (The ship struck the Carrack Shoal, and a stranding finding would name the shoal; but the place of the act that lost her, if she was scuttled, is the engine room, not the shoal. The shoal is where she touched. The engine room is where she was sunk.)

The decisive minute. The time at which the decisive act occurred, in hours and minutes on the 24-hour clock, in the form HH:MM, ship's time on the night of 14 November 1924, with a leading zero for hours under ten and the colon required. The decisive minute is the minute the act that lost the ship was done, not the minute she struck nor the minute she was abandoned. On the evidence (file 06), the act was done in the few minutes after the striking, while the engine-room watch was the second engineer's and before the chief came below, and the inquiry fixes it at about 23:15. The assessor should enter the minute the evidence centres the act upon, which is not the 23:10 of the striking nor the 23:40 of the abandon order.

## Preconditions for entering the finding

Two preconditions must be satisfied before the case office will receive the finding.

The first is the precondition of a complete pass. The assessor must have worked the file through at least once in full, having drawn and examined the records and read the depositions, before a finding will be entertained. A finding entered upon a first and partial reading will be refused as premature. (See file 02 for the procedure of the pass and how the office knows it complete.)

The second is the precondition of the required records. The assessor must have, in the working file at the moment the finding is entered, all of the records the office requires. The authoritative list is the office's own statement of the required records, given in the records log (file 07) and confirmed here; for the present inquiry it is:

- rec-salvage-diver
- rec-cargo-tally
- rec-policy
- rec-owner-letters
- rec-manifest

If any of these records is missing from the working file at the moment the finding is entered, the office will refuse the finding as evidentially incomplete. The deck log, the boiler-room report, the weather and wireless logs, the load-line certificate, and the depositions are held and may be drawn and examined, and several reward it, but they are not on the required list; take the required list from the office's own statement of it, not from any passing mention elsewhere in the file.

## The office's response

The office, on receiving a finding, will return its adjudication: whether the finding is sound or unsound, and, if unsound, in which particular. A sound finding is returned only when the four particulars (party, means, place, and minute) are all correct, all the required records are in the working file, and the precondition of a complete pass is satisfied. An unsound finding is returned with a note of which particular was wrong, which record was missing, or which precondition was unmet, so far as the office gives that note, and the assessor may consider it in framing a further finding where the procedure allows.

## One finding to the inquiry

The inquiry admits one finding. Once a finding has been entered, the matter is concluded so far as that entry goes, and a further finding cannot be entered against the same inquiry. To put a different finding the assessor must open the inquiry afresh and work the file through again. The office takes this position deliberately: a finding is a formal and final act of the assessor, the conclusion of the inquiry, not a guess to be repeated until it lands.

The assessor should therefore enter a finding only when sure, having drawn and examined the required records, completed at least one full pass of the file, and reasoned the cause through from the whole of the evidence and not from the surface of it.

## The particulars, illustrated

It may help to set out, abstractly, the two findings the file makes possible, so that the assessor sees clearly what each of the four particulars would be under each, and chooses with his eyes open. The assessor enters one finding, of four particulars; he does not enter both, and he does not hedge.

The finding the surface of the file would press is, in its four particulars: the party, the master (the only party the file accuses); the means, a stranding in fog; the place, the Carrack Shoal (where she struck); the minute, 23:10 (when she struck). This is the finding of an honest accident with the navigation at fault. It is the finding the preliminary inquiry reached and the finding the arranger of the loss intended the file to yield. The assessor should enter it only if the records bear it, and the records, read together, do not: a stranding does not beat sound pumps, open a sea-cock undamaged, or wire the bilge valves shut, and the master neither gained by the loss nor could have arranged it.

The finding the records bear is, in its four particulars: the party, the one party who had the interest in the loss and the power to over-insure the ship, falsify the cargo, and engage and instruct the man below (try the seven against the four requirements in file 03, and one alone survives); the means, the scuttling of the ship by the open sea-cock, scuttle-seacock; the place, the engine room, loc-engine-room (where the sea inlet and the bilge manifold are, and where the act was done); the minute, about 23:15 (in the interval after the striking, while the engine room was the second engineer's alone). This is the finding of a deliberate loss. It is the finding the assessor should enter if, and only if, he has tested each of its particulars against the records and found them borne out, which is the work the inquiry exists to do.

The two findings differ in every particular: in the party, the means, the place, and the minute. They are not variations of one finding but opposite findings, and the difference between them is the difference between an accident and a crime. The assessor must not blur them. He must determine, from the records, which is true, and enter that one, in all four of its particulars, exactly.

A caution on the place and the minute in particular, for these are the particulars most easily got wrong even by an assessor who has the party and the means right. The place is not the shoal but the engine room: the shoal is where the ship touched, the engine room is where she was sunk, and a finding of scuttling that names the shoal as the place has confused the touching with the sinking. The minute is not the striking and not the abandon order, both of which are logged and tempting; it is the minute of the act, in the interval between them, which the timeline (file 06) fixes at about 23:15. An assessor who names 23:10 has named the accident; one who names 23:40 has named the moment the bridge learned of the doom, not the moment of the act. Name the place and the minute of the act itself.

## After a sound finding

A sound finding closes the inquiry. The assessor's report is filed with the underwriters and the Board of Trade. The party found responsible is named, the insurances are dealt with accordingly, and the matter passes out of the assessor's hands.

Enter the finding when, and only when, the evidence has made it sure. The file leans one way; the records, read together, lean another. Your finding is owed to the records.
