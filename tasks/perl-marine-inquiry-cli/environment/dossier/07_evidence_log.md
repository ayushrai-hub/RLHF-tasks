# Records Log

This is the working catalogue of every record before the inquiry: each log, report, certificate, deposition, schedule, and bundle of papers, with a note of what it is, where it is held, what it establishes, what it does not establish, and the weight the inquiry presently gives it. The longer records (the salvage diver's report, the policy schedules, the surveyor's tally, the owner's letters) are reproduced in full in the appendices (file 09); this log gives their substance and their bearing.

Each record carries a short reference of the form rec-something. That reference is the form in which the record is drawn from the case office and entered in your working file, and the form in which the office will look for it when you come to enter your finding. Draw each record, examine it, and confirm that the reference the office associates with it matches the reference in this log.

The records are grouped by what they bear upon. Within each group they are listed with the reference, the holding, the substance, and the analysis. Read the analysis as the inquiry's working note, not as its finding.

## Group A: the manner of the loss (the physical state of the wreck)

### rec-salvage-diver — the salvage diver's report

Holding: the underwriters' file; reproduced in full in file 09.

Substance: the report of the salvage diver engaged by the underwriters to descend upon the wreck of the Marisol where she lies, and to examine and report upon the state of her hull and her engine-room fittings, in particular the main sea inlet (the sea-cock) and the bilge pumping arrangements. The diver made two descents and reported in writing, with sketches.

The decisive passages of the report are these. **The main sea inlet valve (the sea-cock) was found wide open, the valve hard back against its stop, with no fracture, no distortion, and no damage to the valve, the spindle, or the seating that could account for its being open; it had been opened by hand, deliberately, and not started by the stranding.** And further: **the bilge suction valves on the engine-room manifold were found shut and secured shut with turns of soft iron wire passed through the handwheels and round the standard, so that the pumps could not have been put on the flooding compartments; this wiring was the work of a hand, done before the flooding, and is in no way a consequence of the grounding or the foundering.** The diver adds that he found no damage to the hull plating forward or in way of the holds consistent with a heavy grounding; such bottom damage as there was, was slight, and wholly insufficient of itself to have sunk the ship; the ship was filled, in his opinion, through the open sea inlet, the pumps being held off her by the wired valves.

Analysis: this is the record that distinguishes an accident from an act. A ship that strands and fills by her grounding shows a torn or started bottom and a sea inlet in its working state. This ship showed a sound enough bottom, a sea inlet wide open by a deliberate hand with no damage to account for it, and the bilge valves wired shut so the pumps were useless. There is no reading of this state consistent with an honest stranding. The sea was let into the Marisol on purpose, low down in her engine room, and the means of getting it out again was deliberately disabled. The diver does not say by whom. He says by what, and that it was by a hand and not by the sea.

What it does not tell us: the identity of the hand. The diver found the fittings; he did not find the man. Who was below at the sea inlet and the manifold in the minutes after the striking is established by the depositions (file 04, the fireman Geary; the chief engineer Such), not by the diver.

This is one of the records the office requires in your working file before it will receive a finding.

### rec-boiler-report — the boiler-room report

Holding: the underwriters' file.

Substance: the report compiled from the surviving stokehold and engine-room hands as to the state of the machinery, the steam, and the pumps on the night of the loss, and the sequence of events below from the striking to the abandonment of the engine room.

Analysis: the report establishes that both boilers were in use and steam was up; that the bilge pumps, the main pump and the independent Worthington steam pump, were both in good order and ample for the ship; that water came into the stokehold from the engine-room side very soon after the striking; and that the engine room was abandoned when the water rose past control. It corroborates the chief engineer's account that sound and ample pumps were wholly beaten, which (set beside the salvage diver's finding) they would be, drawing on wired-shut valves against an open sea inlet.

What it does not tell us: it does not, of itself, name the cause of the inrush; it records the effect below. The cause is in rec-salvage-diver.

This is not one of the required records, but it bears directly on the manner of the loss and should be examined.

## Group B: what was, and was not, in the holds

### rec-cargo-tally — the cargo surveyor's tally

Holding: the cargo sub-underwriter's file; reproduced in full in file 09.

Substance: the certified tally made by Miss Hester Cardew, independent cargo surveyor, of the goods actually loaded aboard the Marisol at Liverpool on the 11th and 12th of November, item by item, against the manifest. The tally is signed and dated and Miss Cardew stands by it (her deposition, file 04).

The decisive entry is this. Against the manifest's principal item, the consignment of machine tools, two hundred and forty cases, the tally bears the surveyor's note: **"Machine tools, 240 cases: NOT SEEN LOADED. None of this parcel passed over the quayside hatch under my tally, where all the rest of the cargo was worked. I was directed away from the offshore side, where I was told the firm's own lighters would strike this parcel in, and was prevented from verifying it. I cannot certify that any part of the declared consignment of machine tools came aboard this ship."** The rest of the tally, the cork, the tin plate, the sundries, the empties, answers to the manifest and is certified loaded; only the machine tools, the one item that gave the cargo its declared value, is marked not seen and not certified.

Analysis: this is the record that empties the holds. The manifest (rec-manifest) declares a rich cargo whose whole value is in the machine tools. The surveyor's tally certifies everything but the machine tools and records that the machine tools were never seen aboard and that she was kept from the place they could otherwise have gone. Taken with the ship's trim on departure (down by the head, ballasted aft, the trim of a ship that has not that weight low in her after holds, see the mate's deposition and the load-line certificate) and with the absence from the firm's own papers of any charter, consignee, or freight for the parcel (file 05), the tally establishes that the declared machine tools were not aboard. What lay in numbers two and three in their place, ballast and low-value goods, the wreck and the diver's notes suggest; that they were not the declared tools, the tally settles.

What it does not tell us: the tally does not, of itself, say who falsified the cargo. It says what was and was not loaded. The declaration of the cargo, and its falsity, is the act of the man who made or approved the declaration and the insurance upon it (file 03, file 05), not of the surveyor who counted.

This is one of the records the office requires in your working file before it will receive a finding.

### rec-manifest — the cargo manifest

Holding: the firm's papers; reproduced in part in file 09.

Substance: the official manifest of the Marisol's cargo for the voyage, as declared by the firm. It lists the cargo item by item with declared quantities and values. The principal item is the consignment of machine tools, 240 cases, with a declared value running to many thousands of pounds, for stowage in numbers two and three holds; the remainder (cork, tin plate, sundries, empties) is of small value by comparison.

Analysis: the manifest is the firm's declaration of what the ship carried, and it is the document against which the surveyor's tally is read and the cargo insurance declared. Standing alone, it is an ordinary manifest. Standing beside rec-cargo-tally, it is the declaration of a cargo whose most valuable item was never loaded; the manifest asserts the tools aboard, the tally certifies they were not. The whole declared value of the cargo, and the whole of the cargo insurance, rests on the one item the surveyor did not see loaded.

What it does not tell us: the manifest asserts; it does not prove loading. Proof of loading is the surveyor's office, and her tally contradicts the manifest on the decisive item.

This is one of the records the office requires in your working file before it will receive a finding. It is required precisely so that it may be read against the tally; a finding on the cargo cannot be sound without both before it.

## Group C: the motive (what she and her cargo were insured for)

### rec-policy — the policy schedule

Holding: the underwriters' file, produced by Mr Mercer; reproduced in full in file 09.

Substance: the schedule of the insurances on the Marisol, hull and cargo. It gives the sum insured on the hull and machinery; the declared value and sum insured on the cargo; the dates on which the covers were placed and on which the cargo declaration was increased; and the names of the assured and of those to whose order the insurance money would be paid upon a loss.

Analysis: this is the record that measures the motive. The hull of a twenty-six-year-old steamer, of a class for which the 1924 market was very poor, was insured for a sum far above her market value and a great multiple of her break-up value (compare the owner's own letter of 9 October, file 05, calling her worth a third of her cover, and the load-line and survey particulars in file 09). The cargo was insured by declaration to its full figure, the bulk of that figure being the machine tools, the cover increased to the full figure only two days before sailing (Mr Mercer's deposition, file 04; the firm's policy notes, file 05). Upon a total loss, the whole of the insurance money, hull and cargo alike, would come to the firm of Veil and Penhallow, that is to the managing owner, or to his order. The schedule thus shows a very large sum payable upon the loss of an old ship and a cargo whose most valuable part (by the tally) was never aboard, the whole payable to the one man who controlled the ship, the declaration, and the insurances.

What it does not tell us: the schedule shows the figures and the payee. It does not, of itself, say the loss was procured to collect them. That inference is for the assessor, drawing the schedule together with the manner of the loss (Group A), the falsity of the cargo (Group B), and the owner's correspondence (rec-owner-letters).

This is one of the records the office requires in your working file before it will receive a finding.

### rec-owner-letters — the owner's correspondence

Holding: the firm's letter-book and private memoranda, produced under compulsion; reproduced and extracted in file 05 and file 09.

Substance: the correspondence and private papers of the firm touching the insurances, the cargo declaration, and the engagement and instruction of the second engineer, Lund. It includes the owner's letters to the broker (placing the high hull cover; raising the cargo declaration to its full figure two days before sailing); to the master (directing him to leave the valuable tools parcel wholly to the firm and to sign for it unseen); to the quay agent (directing that the independent surveyor be kept from the offshore side where the tools were said to be loaded); and to the second engineer (engaging him as the owner's man, paying him several times his wage on a "special" account with no service named, and instructing him in a private letter, marked to be burned, to see to the main injection and the bilge gear, low down and aft and quickly, before the pumps could be got on her, once the ship was on the foul ground in the fog).

Analysis: this is the record that joins the motive to the act and names the hand that arranged both. No one letter confesses anything; the owner was careful. But the correspondence, read in its order and against the records, is the arrangement of the loss: the over-insurance of the hull, the inflation of the cargo cover on a parcel for which there is no charter or freight in the firm's own papers, the keeping of the independent surveyor from the loading, the master kept ignorant and made to sign for a cargo he did not see, and the owner's own engineer paid and privately instructed to do, low down in the engine room, exactly the thing the salvage diver found done. The acts the letters describe are the acts the physical evidence proves were carried out. The hand that wrote the letters is the hand that arranged the loss.

What it does not tell us: the letters do not name the crime in terms, and the most explicit of them was meant to be burned and survives only as a letter-book copy produced under compulsion. The assessor must read the letters with the engineering evidence, the tally, and the schedule, and draw the conclusion the letters were written to avoid stating. The letters establish the arrangement and its author; they leave the naming of the thing to the assessor.

This is one of the records the office requires in your working file before it will receive a finding.

## Group D: the navigation and the night (the logs and certificates)

### rec-deck-log — the deck log

Holding: recovered with the master's effects; reproduced in part in file 09.

Substance: the bridge log of the voyage, with the entries for the 14th: the thickening fog, the reduction of speed, the soundings, the striking at 23:10, the order to abandon at 23:40. It fixes the two logged minutes of the night, the striking and the abandon order, between which the decisive act lies.

Analysis: the deck log is the navigational record and the timekeeper of the night. It fixes the striking and the abandonment and supports the master's account of careful navigation in fog (reduction of speed, soundings, lookouts). It does not, and cannot, record what was done below in the engine room; the bridge did not know. The reader takes from the deck log the hours of the night, and in particular the striking at 23:10 and the abandon order at 23:40, and reads the interval between them with the engineering evidence (file 06).

What it does not tell us: the cause of the filling, and the decisive act below, which the bridge could not see and the log does not record.

This is not a required record, but it fixes the timeline and should be examined.

### rec-weather-log — the weather log

Holding: recovered with the master's effects.

Substance: the record of wind, weather, and barometer through the voyage, confirming the thickening fog of the 14th and the light winds, and giving the conditions in which the ship ran onto the shoal.

Analysis: the weather log establishes that the fog was genuine and thick and that the night was the kind of night on which a ship may be set in by current unseen, which supports the master against the charge of recklessness and supplies, equally, the cover of fog under which the loss was procured. The same fog that excuses the master's navigation is the fog the owner's letter to Lund counted upon ("the fog lies thick at this season"). The weather was real; what was done under cover of it was not the weather's doing.

What it does not tell us: nothing of the cause of the filling.

This is not a required record. It is corroborative of the conditions.

### rec-wireless-log — the wireless log

Holding: recovered; reproduced in part in file 09.

Substance: the record of wireless traffic through the voyage and the night: routine exchanges, the absence of any distress call before the abandonment, the absence of any warning received as to the ship's position.

Analysis: the wireless log establishes that nothing in the traffic touched the navigation or warned of the shoal, and that no distress was sent until the end. It neither helps nor hinders the cause; it is catalogued for completeness and to close the question whether any warning was had and ignored. None was.

What it does not tell us: anything of the cause of the loss.

This is not a required record. It is essentially neutral.

### rec-loadline-cert — the load-line certificate

Holding: the firm's papers and the survey file; reproduced in part in file 09.

Substance: the load-line (freeboard) certificate then current for the Marisol, with the particulars of her last special survey, her assigned freeboard, and her condition as surveyed.

Analysis: the certificate bears upon two matters. It confirms the ship's age and condition (an old ship, lately surveyed, of small value), which goes to the over-insurance of the hull (read with rec-policy). And the freeboard and draught particulars, set against the ship's trim on departure (down by the head, ballasted aft, per the mate), confirm that she did not sail with the weight of the declared tools low in her after holds; a ship loaded with 240 cases of machine tools in numbers two and three would have floated to a different mark and trim than the one she sailed on.

What it does not tell us: it does not name the cause of the loss; it goes to the ship's value and her trim.

This is not a required record, but it corroborates both the over-insurance and the absence of the tools.

## Group E: the depositions, as records

The depositions (file 04) are also recorded here as records the office holds, for the assessor who wishes to draw and examine them by reference. They are listed for completeness; their substance is in file 04.

### rec-frane-deposition — deposition of Captain Frane

The master's account of the navigation, the striking, the filling, and the abandonment. Exonerates the navigation so far as fog and current allow, and establishes that the master abandoned a ship he believed past saving by a cause he did not know. Not a required record; central to the exoneration of the master.

### rec-lund-deposition — (no deposition; record of the second engineer's absence)

There is no deposition of the second engineer, Lund, who was lost. This reference holds, in his place, the record of his engagement, his pay, and his last-seen movements below (the firm's articles and cash book, file 05; the fireman Geary's account, file 04), gathered for the assessor under the second engineer's name. Not a required record; bears on the hand that did the act.

### rec-okonkwo-deposition — deposition of First Mate Okonkwo

The mate's account of the loading (the tools not seen worked, the wrong trim, the firm's men and lighters) and of the night (the light striking, the fast filling). Not a required record; corroborates the tally and the manner of the loss.

### rec-survivor-accounts — the survivors' accounts

The collected accounts of the surviving crew, including the fireman Geary's, as to the abandonment and the movements below. Not a required record; the fireman's account places Lund at the sea inlet in the decisive minutes.

## Summary of the records

The five records the office requires in your working file before it will receive a finding are:

- rec-salvage-diver — the main sea inlet found wide open by a deliberate hand and the bilge valves wired shut; the ship filled on purpose, not by grounding.
- rec-cargo-tally — the declared machine tools never seen loaded; the cargo's whole declared value was in a parcel that did not come aboard.
- rec-policy — the hull insured far above its worth and the cargo declared to the hilt two days before sailing, the whole payable to the owner on a total loss.
- rec-owner-letters — the over-insurance, the false cargo, the surveyor kept off, the master kept ignorant, and the owner's own engineer paid and privately instructed to open the sea low down and aft before the pumps could be got on her.
- rec-manifest — the declaration of the cargo, required to be read against the tally that contradicts it on the decisive item.

These five, read together, establish the manner of the loss (Group A), the falsity of the cargo (Group B), the motive (Group C), and the arrangement and its author (rec-owner-letters). The other records (the boiler-room report, the deck, weather, and wireless logs, the load-line certificate, the depositions) corroborate, fix the hours, or exclude alternatives; none is required for the formal finding, but several reward examination, and the boiler-room report and the depositions in particular bear directly on the cause and on the men.

Draw and examine all five required records, and as many of the others as the case requires, before you enter your finding. Do not enter a finding before you have completed at least one full pass of the file. Be prepared, on entering it, to name the party responsible, the means of the loss, the place of the loss, and the decisive minute.
