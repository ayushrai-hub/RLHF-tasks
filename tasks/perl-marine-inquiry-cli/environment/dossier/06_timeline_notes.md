# Reconstructed Timeline of the Loss

What follows is the inquiry's reconstructed timeline of the night of 14 November 1924, from the thickening of the fog in the afternoon to the foundering in the small hours of the 15th, drawn from the deck log, the wireless log, the boiler-room report, the depositions of the survivors, and the salvage diver's later examination of the wreck. The times are ship's time, kept on board, and are given in the 24-hour clock. Where a time is fixed by a record it is so noted; where it is an estimate from the evidence it is marked as such.

The timeline is the inquiry's best current account. It is not a finding. Several of its hours remain open, and at each the contradictions and the unresolved points are noted at the end. The reader should hold in mind, throughout, the single fact that the timeline cannot of itself explain: that a ship which took the ground as gently as the Marisol took it, by the master's and the mate's account, was filling past her pumps within minutes and foundering within hours, which no honest grounding of that kind should produce. The hours below record what happened. Why the water came as it came is the matter the records in file 07 and file 09 must settle.

A note on the decisive minute. This inquiry must fix not only the cause of the loss but the time of the act that caused it. If the loss was an honest stranding, the decisive minute is the minute she struck. If the loss was procured from below, by the admission of the sea low down in the engine room, the decisive minute is the minute that was done, which the evidence places in the few minutes after she struck, while the watch was the second engineer's and before the chief engineer came below. The reader should attend to that short interval with particular care, for it is there, if anywhere, that the loss of the Marisol was made certain.

---

## Afternoon and early evening, 14 November

By the deck log, the weather thickened from about 15:00, the glass steady, the wind light. Fog set in patchily by 17:00 and thickened through the dog watches. The master reduced from full to half speed at about 20:00 (deck log) and posted extra lookouts. The lead was made ready and the ship sounded twice in the evening, getting no bottom at the depths sounded, consistent with the reckoning that she was well to seaward of the Carrack Shoal.

The wireless log records ordinary traffic through the evening, a routine exchange with a shore station at 21:40, nothing touching the navigation, no distress, no warning of the ship's position received or sent. The boiler-room report records steam maintained for half speed, fires worked normally, both boilers in use.

Open points for this period: none of moment. The ship was being navigated with ordinary care in thick fog on a known coast, by every account. The only matter to carry forward is the inset current the master speaks of, by which he believes the ship was set in toward the land in the fog beyond what the reckoning showed. The deck log records no observed set, the fog preventing any fix; the current is the master's later inference, not a logged fact.

---

## 22:00 to 23:00

By the deck log the ship ran on at half speed through thickening fog. Lookouts forward and on the bridge wings. The master and the first mate, Mr Okonkwo, were both on the bridge. A further sounding was attempted; the record is unclear whether bottom was got. The chief engineer, Mr Such, had gone below to his bunk at about 22:00, the engine-room watch being kept by the second engineer, Mr Lund. The boiler-room report and the surviving fireman, Geary, place Lund in the engine room from the start of his watch.

Open points for this period: the position of the ship. By her own reckoning she was outside the shoal. By the event she was not, for she struck within the hour. The discrepancy is the heart of the navigational question. The master attributes it to the inset current; the Board of Trade attributed it to slack navigation. The inquiry notes only that the ship was where the reckoning did not put her, and that whether she came there by current, by error, or by a course steered toward the foul ground, the timeline cannot of itself say. The reader should weigh the master's account of the run-in (file 04) against the suggestion, nowhere in this file made out but nowhere excluded, that a ship may be brought to a known patch of foul ground in known seasonal fog by intention as readily as by mischance.

---

## 23:10 — the striking (deck log)

The deck log fixes the striking at 23:10. The master rang the engine to stop and to stand by. The striking, by the master's and the mate's concurrent account, was light: a single shock and then the ship held forward, not driven hard up. The master expected to lie on the ground until the tide made and to float off, and gave his first orders accordingly, to sound round the ship and get the carpenter to the wells.

This is the minute on which an honest-accident finding would rest its time of loss. The reader should mark it, and then mark what follows, and ask whether the minute the ship struck and the minute her loss was made certain are the same minute or two different ones.

Open points for this period: the severity of the striking against the rate of filling. Two officers of experience judged the striking light, the kind of grounding a sound ship rides out. The filling that followed was not the filling such a grounding produces. The contradiction is logged here and not resolved here. It is resolved, if at all, by the engineering evidence and the salvage diver's report.

---

## 23:10 to 23:25 — the engine room, the watch of the second engineer

This is the short interval the reader was bid to attend to, and it is the interval the timeline can least securely fill, because the one man who held the engine room through it did not survive and gave no account.

What the records and the survivors establish is this. From the striking at 23:10 until the chief engineer reached the engine room, the engine room was in the charge of the second engineer, Lund, alone but for such firemen as were at the stokehold end. The master rang down after the striking and got no clear answer from the engine room (master's deposition). The fireman Geary, ordered up as the water began to come into the stokehold from the engine-room side, looked back as he went up the ladder and saw Lund not coming up with the rest but going down and aft, low down by the plates, toward the after end of the engine room where the main sea inlet and the bilge manifold are, and working at something there in the rising water (Geary's deposition, file 04). Geary places this at the time the water was first coming strongly into the stokehold, which on the boiler-room report and his own account was very few minutes after the striking. He was among the last up and Lund was still below when he lost sight of him.

The chief engineer, Such, roused by the striking, dressed and came below, reaching the engine room (by his estimate) some minutes after the striking, perhaps eight or ten, the water already over the plates and rising past anything he had seen. He found, when he reached the bilge manifold, certain of the bilge valves shut that should have been open, and could not get the pumps to draw to any effect; and he judged, from the manner of the water, a fierce live inrush low down, that the sea was coming in by a large opening under pressure and not by a grounding leak (Such's deposition, file 04). He had no time to examine the sea inlet itself before he had to get his men up.

The inquiry therefore fixes the decisive act, the opening of the sea to the ship low down and the securing of the bilge valves against the pumps, within this interval, and on the best reading of the evidence at about 23:15, that is, within five minutes of the striking, while the watch was Lund's and before the chief came below. The salvage diver's later finding (file 07, file 09), of the main sea inlet wide open and the bilge valves wired shut, is the physical confirmation of what was done in this interval; it could not have been done after, the engine room being abandoned to the sea, nor before, the ship being then in open water and afloat. The window is the window between the striking and the chief's arrival, and the act sits at the front of it, at or about 23:15.

Open points for this period: the exact minute, which no clock recorded, the engine-room watch having other concerns and the watchkeeper being the man who did the thing. The inquiry's 23:15 is an estimate bounded below by the striking at 23:10 and above by the chief's arrival; the evidence will not narrow it further, and the reader who must name a minute should name the minute the evidence centres on, in the few minutes after the striking, while the engine room was Lund's alone. A finding that named the minute of the striking itself would be naming the accident, not the act; the act came after the striking, in this interval, by a hand below.

---

## A closer reconstruction of the engine-room interval

Because the decisive minute lies in this interval, and because no clock recorded it and no survivor of the engine room itself came up to tell it, the inquiry has reconstructed the interval as closely as the evidence allows, that the assessor may see how the minute of about 23:15 is arrived at and why it cannot be the 23:10 of the striking.

At 23:10 the ship struck, lightly, and the bridge rang stop and stand by. At that instant the engine room was the second engineer's: the chief was in his bunk, roused by the shock but not yet dressed and below. For some minutes, then, the second engineer had the engine room substantially to himself, the firemen being at the stokehold end forward of him, knocking back their fires on the stand-by order. Those few minutes, from about 23:10 to about 23:18, are the only window in the whole night in which the sea inlet could have been opened and the bilge valves secured without another responsible man at hand to see it done; before the striking the ship was afloat in open water and there was nothing to be gained by opening her, and after the chief came below the engine room was no longer the second engineer's alone.

The boiler-room report and the firemen place the first strong inrush of water into the stokehold from the engine-room side at very few minutes after the striking, and it was that inrush that sent the firemen to the ladders. As they went, both Geary and Tasker saw the second engineer not coming up but going down and aft to the sea inlet and the bilge gear, and working there in the rising water. Water does not pour into a stokehold from the engine-room side, within a few minutes of a light grounding, from a grounding leak forward; it does so when the sea inlet aft in the engine room has been opened. The inrush the firemen felt is therefore itself evidence that the sea inlet was already open by the time they went up, which was a few minutes after the striking. The opening of it must thus be placed in the first minutes after 23:10 and before that inrush drove the firemen out, that is, at about 23:15.

The chief engineer reached the manifold some minutes later, by his estimate eight or ten after the striking, so about 23:18 to 23:20, and found the bilge valves already wrong and the water already past holding. The securing of the valves was therefore also done before he arrived, in the same early interval. Everything the survivors establish converges on the same short window: the act was done in the first five minutes or so after the striking, while the engine room was the second engineer's alone, and the inquiry fixes it at about 23:15.

The assessor who must name a single minute should name 23:15, as the minute the evidence centres the act upon; it cannot be 23:10, which is the minute of the striking and of the accident, not of the act; and it cannot be 23:40, which is the minute of the abandon order, long after the ship was already doomed. The act that lost her sits between the two logged minutes, near the front of the interval, at about 23:15.

## 23:25 to 23:40 — the water gains

By all accounts the water gained fast through this period. The carpenter reported water in number three hold and the engine room (master's deposition). The chief engineer got both pumps on the bilge main and they made no impression. The master rang down repeatedly. The ship began to settle by the stern, the engine room being the deepest-flooded compartment, which of itself tells against a grounding leak forward and for an inflow low down amidships and aft.

Open points: none of substance. The water was winning and every man aboard could see it. The only question the period raises is the one already raised: water winning so fast and so far aft is not the signature of the grounding the ship suffered.

---

## 23:40 — the order to abandon (deck log)

The master gave the order to abandon at 23:40 (deck log), the water being over the engine-room plates and rising, the pumps beaten, and the chief engineer's report being that he could not hold her and could not understand why. The master's judgment was that the ship would not float and that to keep his people aboard was to drown them.

The reader will note that the rumour against the master fixes on this minute: that he abandoned too soon. The timeline records that he abandoned a ship that was, on the engineering evidence, already past saving by a cause he did not know, a ship into which the sea was pouring by an opening no pump could match. A master cannot be faulted for abandoning a ship that is filling past her pumps by a sea-cock he does not know is open. The minute of the abandon order is a real minute and a logged one, but it is not the decisive minute of the loss; the loss was made certain twenty-five minutes earlier, below, while this master was on his bridge expecting to float off on the tide.

Open points: whether the abandonment was premature. The inquiry's view, on the engineering evidence, is that it was not: the ship was past saving from about the time the sea-cock was opened, and the master's order, late in the event and forced by a cause hidden from him, was the only order open to him. The five lives were lost in the fouling of the after boat in the launching, a misadventure of the launching and not of the timing of the order.

---

## Midnight to the small hours — the foundering

The two midship boats got away near midnight. The after boat fouled in launching and was lost with five men. The ship settled by the stern through the small hours and slipped off the shoal on the falling tide into deep water, where she foundered. The survivors were taken up the following forenoon by the coaster Estremoz and landed at Oporto.

Open points: none touching the cause. The foundering followed inevitably from the flooding. The wreck now lies in deep water and was examined by the salvage diver some weeks later; his report (file 07, file 09) is the physical record of the state of the sea inlet and the bilge valves, taken from the wreck herself.

---

## The two readings of the night, set side by side

The same hours admit of more than one reading, and the assessor's task is to determine which reading the records bear. The timeline itself does not decide between them; it lays out the hours and the open points, and leaves the cause to be settled from file 07. It may help, all the same, to set the two readings out plainly, so that the assessor knows what each requires.

The first reading is the preliminary inquiry's. A careful enough master in the worst fog of the season is set in toward the land by a current he cannot see. His ship touches the Carrack ground at 23:10. The grounding, though it felt light, opens her below; old ships are tender, and a tender old ship may be hurt worse than her master feels. She makes water; the water beats the pumps because the damage is greater than anyone aboard supposes; she fills and is abandoned at 23:40 and founders. The loss is an honest stranding in fog, with perhaps a word of criticism for the navigation that brought her to the shoal. On this reading the decisive minute is 23:10, for that is the minute the sea began to take her.

The second reading is the one the assessor must test against the records, and adopt only if the records bear it. On this reading the ship touches lightly at 23:10, taking only slight damage; and then, in the few minutes after she strikes, while the engine room is the second engineer's alone, the sea is admitted to her low down and the pumps disabled, so that she fills past all holding from a cause no man on the bridge can see. On this reading the decisive minute is not 23:10 but a minute in the interval after it, about 23:15, when the act was done. Whether this reading is the true one is not for the timeline to say. It turns on whether the slight damage the diver found could have beaten sound pumps; on whether the sea inlet was open of itself or by a hand; on whether the bilge valves were shut by accident or secured; on whether the cargo that gave the ship her value was aboard or not; and on what the owner's papers show was arranged and instructed before she sailed. None of those questions is answered by a clock or a deck log. Each is answered by a record in file 07, and the assessor must answer each from the record before he adopts either reading.

The two readings turn on the same facts and differ only in the cause; and the cause is the whole of the case. The assessor should not choose between them by which is the more comfortable or the more usual, but by which the records compel. Read the records, and let them, and not this timeline, decide.

## Summary of the timeline, and what it cannot settle

The timeline establishes, from the records: that the ship was navigated with ordinary care in thick fog; that she struck the Carrack Shoal at 23:10, lightly; that within a very few minutes she was filling past her pumps; that the second engineer was below at the sea inlet and bilge gear in those minutes while every other man went up, and did not himself come up; that the chief engineer found the bilge valves wrong and the water coming in like an opened sea-cock; that the master abandoned at 23:40, the ship being then past saving; and that she foundered in deep water in the small hours.

The timeline cannot, of itself, settle why she filled. That is not within the gift of a clock and a deck log. It is within the gift of the engineering evidence, the salvage diver's report, the surveyor's tally of what was truly in her holds, the policy schedule of what she and her cargo were insured for, and the owner's correspondence. The reader who has fixed the hours from this timeline must take the cause, and the responsibility, and the decisive minute, from those records, read together. The timeline gives the minute the ship struck and the minute she was abandoned. The minute that lost her lies between them, at about 23:15, below, and the records will show by whose hand and at whose instance it was done.
