## Appendix I — Salvage diver's report (rec-salvage-diver), in full

Report of survey of the wreck of the SS Marisol, made on behalf of the underwriters by the undersigned salvage diver, after two descents upon the wreck where she lies in deep water off the Carrack Shoal.

I descended upon the wreck on two occasions. She lies upright, somewhat by the stern, on a soft bottom, in deep water. I was instructed to examine and report upon the state of her hull in way of the holds and the engine room, and in particular upon the main sea inlet (the sea-cock) and the bilge pumping arrangements in the engine room, and to bring up such observations as I could.

Of the hull: I examined the bottom plating forward and in way of numbers one, two, and three holds, so far as the lie of the wreck and the bottom allowed. I found no fracture, no set-in, and no opening of the plating consistent with a heavy grounding. There is some slight damage forward, a set-in plate and a started seam in way of number one, such as a ship would take in touching the ground lightly; it is slight, and it is well above the turn of the bilge, and it is in my opinion wholly insufficient of itself to have admitted water enough to sink the ship, the pumps being able to hold such a leak with ease. The ship was not opened up by her grounding.

Of the main sea inlet: I made my way to the engine room and found the main sea inlet valve, the sea-cock, **wide open, the valve hard back against its stop. I examined the valve, the spindle, and the seating closely and found no fracture, no distortion, and no damage of any kind that could account for its being open; it had not been started open by the stranding nor by the foundering; it had been opened by hand, deliberately, and set hard open, and so I found it.** A sea inlet in the working of the ship stands part open to the condenser and the pumps; this valve was not part open to any service but wide open to the sea, hard against its stop, with the bore clear and nothing to receive the water. The sea came into this ship through that valve.

Of the bilge pumping arrangements: I examined the bilge suction manifold in the engine room. **I found the bilge suction valves shut, and not merely shut but secured shut, turns of soft iron wire having been passed through the spokes of the handwheels and made fast round the manifold standard, so that the valves could not be opened without first casting off the wire. The wire was in place and made fast when I found it. This securing was done by a hand, of set purpose, before the flooding; it is in no way a consequence of the grounding or of the sinking, and no accident could have wired those wheels shut.** With the suction valves so secured, the bilge pumps, however worked, could draw on nothing and could throw no water out of the flooding compartments.

I report, in conclusion, that the Marisol was not sunk by her grounding, which did her only slight damage well within her pumps; that she was sunk by the admission of the sea through the main sea inlet, which I found wide open by a deliberate hand with no damage to account for it; and that the bilge pumps were prevented from holding her by the bilge suction valves, which I found wired shut by a deliberate hand before the flooding. The opening of the one and the securing of the other are, in my experience of wrecks, the unmistakable marks of a ship sunk on purpose from within her own engine room, and not of a ship lost by stranding.

(Sketches of the sea inlet, hard open against its stop, and of the wired manifold wheels, accompany the original.)

## Appendix VIII — Boiler-room and engine-room report (rec-boiler-report), in full

Report compiled by the underwriters from the surviving engine-room and stokehold hands of the Marisol, as to the state of the machinery and the sequence of events below on the night of the loss.

Of the machinery and steam: both boilers in use, steam up for half speed at the time of the striking. Fires worked normally through the evening. The main engine stopped on the order from the bridge at the striking and was not worked again.

Of the pumps: the ship carried two bilge pumps able to draw on the bilge main, the main pump driven off the engine and an independent Worthington-pattern direct-acting steam pump in the engine room. Both were reported by the chief engineer and the surviving hands to have been in good order and of ample capacity for the ship. The independent steam pump could be worked regardless of the main engine and was so worked by the chief engineer after the striking.

Of the bilge manifold: the bilge suction valves are on a manifold in the engine room, by which the several compartments are put on or shut off the pumps. The setting of these valves on the night, as the chief engineer found them when he reached the manifold after the striking, was that certain suctions were shut which should, with the engine room and number three filling, have been open. (The state in which the valves were finally found, wired shut, is in the salvage diver's report, Appendix I; the chief engineer in the dark and the rising water did not at the time perceive the wire.)

Of the sequence below: at the striking (23:10 by the bridge) the engine-room watch was the second engineer's, the chief being in his bunk. The telegraph rang to stop and stand by; the fires were knocked back. Within a few minutes water began to come into the stokehold from the engine-room side, increasing fast. The order came to get the stokehold hands up; they went for the ladders. The fireman Geary, going up, saw the second engineer go down and aft toward the after end of the engine room, low down by the plates in way of the main sea inlet and the bilge gear, working at something in the rising water, and not coming up with the rest; the second engineer was not seen again. The chief engineer came below some minutes after the striking, found the water over the plates and rising past control, got both pumps on the bilge main to no effect, found the manifold suctions wrong and could not free them in the time, and got his remaining men up and out as the engine room flooded.

Conclusion of the report: the machinery and pumps were sound and ample; the engine room nonetheless flooded past control within a very few minutes of a light grounding; the flooding was from low down and aft, in way of the sea inlet and the after compartments, not from a grounding leak forward; and the pumps, though sound and worked, made no impression, consistent with the suctions being shut against them and a large opening admitting the sea. The report does not name a cause beyond what the surviving hands observed; the physical cause is established by the salvage diver (Appendix I).

## Appendix XI — Memorandum on stranding and scuttling (the inquiry's engineering adviser)

The underwriters' engineering adviser furnished the inquiry with the following memorandum, that the assessor, who is not necessarily a marine engineer, may weigh the physical evidence with understanding. It is reproduced in full.

The question put to me is how an assessor may tell, from the state of a wreck and the manner of her filling, whether a ship was lost by stranding or sunk by scuttling. I set out the marks of each.

A ship lost by stranding fills because the ground has opened her hull. The marks of it are damage to the bottom plating: fractures, set-in plates, started seams, in way of where she struck, usually forward and along the bottom. The water comes in through such damage as a welling rise, under the head of the sea outside, and a sound pump of adequate capacity will hold a great deal of such leakage; a ship is not easily sunk by a grounding leak alone unless the damage is very extensive. The sea inlet and the bilge gear, in a stranding, are found in their ordinary working state, for no one has touched them.

A ship sunk by scuttling fills because a hand within her has admitted the sea. The commonest means in a steamer is the opening of the main sea inlet, low in the engine room, which admits the sea under full pressure through a large bore and fills the engine room in minutes; sound pumps cannot hold it. The scuttler will commonly also disable the pumps, by shutting or securing the bilge valves, so that what comes in cannot be got out. The marks of scuttling are therefore these: a hull bottom sound, or damaged only slightly and insufficiently to sink her; the sea inlet found open, and open without fracture or damage to account for it, for it has been opened by hand and not started by the sea; and the bilge valves found shut or secured shut, often wired or lashed, which no accident does. The water, to those aboard, comes in not as a welling rise but as a fierce live inrush low down, too fast and too violent for a grounding, and concentrated in the engine room and the compartments the sea inlet serves, not forward where a grounding would open her.

The two causes are, in their physical marks, opposite and not easily confused by a careful examiner. A stranding shows hull damage and untouched fittings; a scuttling shows sound hull and tampered fittings. Where, as I am told is the case here, the bottom is found sound, the sea inlet wide open and undamaged, and the bilge valves wired shut, the marks are those of scuttling and not of stranding, and no degree of fog or current at the surface alters what the diver found below. Fog and current may explain how a ship came to be where she struck. They cannot explain an open sea-cock and wired pumps. Those are the work of a hand, and the assessor's question, once he accepts them, is not whether the ship was scuttled but whose hand did it and at whose instance.

## Appendix XII — Navigation and chart note

Note furnished to the inquiry on the navigational question.

The Carrack Shoal is charted, marked as foul ground, and given a wide berth by the ordinary track for the Tagus, which passes well to seaward of it. A ship on her proper track would not come near it. The Marisol came onto it. Two explanations are before the inquiry.

The first is the master's: that an inset current, which on that coast in certain weather sets strongly toward the land, carried the ship in past her reckoning in the fog, without a fix being possible. This is a known hazard of the coast and has cast away better-found ships than the Marisol; it is consistent with careful navigation defeated by an unseen set, and the weather log supports the conditions in which such a set occurs.

The second, which the inquiry records as a possibility and not as a finding, is that the ship was brought toward the foul ground deliberately, the fog and the known seasonal current serving as both the means and the cover. The owner's letter to the second engineer (file 05) refers to "the place off the coast where the ground is foul and the fog lies thick at this season" as a place the engineer had "run with me on the chart," which shows that the foul ground in fog was a thing the owner had in mind and had discussed before the voyage. Whether the master was an unwitting instrument of a course shaped toward that ground, or was honestly set in by the current, the navigation note cannot determine; what it can say is that the place of the loss was no random patch of sea but a charted danger that an owner's letter shows was known to and contemplated by the owner before she sailed.

The inquiry draws no conclusion as to the navigation beyond this: that the master's account of the inset current is an honest and sufficient explanation of how a careful man came onto the shoal, and that the owner's foreknowledge of that very ground in that very fog is a fact to be weighed with the rest, against the man ashore and not against the man on the bridge.

## Appendix XIII — Loss-of-life and casualty note

Of the crew of twenty-six, twenty-one were taken up and five were lost. The five were: the second engineer, Edvard Lund, last seen below in the engine room and not recovered; and four hands lost in the fouling of the after boat in the launching, whose names are in the crew list. The bodies of the four boat casualties were recovered; the body of the second engineer was not.

The inquiry notes two matters. First, that four of the five deaths were of the launching of the after boat, a misadventure of the abandonment in darkness and a list, and not of the timing of the master's order, which was given in time to clear the two midship boats cleanly (the survivors Renn and the chief engineer concur). The master is not to be charged with those four deaths as the fruit of a premature order; they were the fruit of a fouled boat in a sinking ship. Second, that the one death not of the boats was the second engineer's, below, at the after end of the engine room, in the rising water, where the survivors place him at the sea inlet and the bilge gear. Whether he was caught by the speed of the flooding he had himself procured, or stayed too long at his work, or could not win back to the ladder against the inrush, the inquiry cannot say; but his death was not the master's doing and was not in the boats, and its place, below at the sea valve, is itself a fact in the case.

## Appendix XIV — The assessor's working questions

The prior assessor left a short list of the questions he had found it useful to put to the file, which is reproduced for the assessor who follows.

1. Could a grounding as light as the master and mate describe have done damage enough to sink a sound ship with sound pumps? (The diver's report and the engineering memorandum answer this: no.)
2. If not a grounding, what admitted the sea? (The diver's report: the main sea inlet, found wide open by a hand.)
3. Why did the pumps not hold her? (The diver's report and the chief engineer: the bilge valves were secured shut.)
4. Who was below at those fittings at the time? (The survivors: the second engineer, the owner's man, who did not come up.)
5. Why should anyone wish the ship sunk? (The policy schedule: she and her cargo were insured for a fortune, far above their worth, payable to the owner.)
6. Was the cargo that gave her that insured value truly aboard? (The surveyor's tally: the machine tools were never seen loaded, and the firm kept the surveyor from the only place they could have gone.)
7. Was the act arranged beforehand, and by whom? (The owner's letters: the over-insurance, the false declaration, the surveyor kept off, the master kept ignorant, and the engineer paid and privately instructed to open the sea before the pumps could be got on her.)
8. Who, of all the parties, had the interest, the power to insure and declare, and the power to engage and instruct the man below, all at once? (The parties file: try each against the four requirements, and see who alone survives.)

The assessor who can answer these eight from the records has the case. The answers are not in any one record; they are in the records read together. That reading is the assessor's work, and the file has been arranged, by those with an interest, to discourage it. Do it anyway.

## Appendix XV — Underwriters' investigator's preliminary memorandum

The underwriters, before appointing the present assessor, had the matter looked into by an investigator of their own, whose preliminary memorandum is reproduced. It is the memorandum that moved the underwriters to open the fuller inquiry. It states facts and discrepancies; it does not pronounce a finding, that being the assessor's office.

To the underwriters. I have looked into the loss of the Marisol so far as a preliminary inquiry allows, and I report the following discrepancies, which in my judgment warrant a full investigation before any claim is paid.

First, as to the hull. The vessel was insured at £58,000. She was a steamer of twenty-six years, of a class for which the market is very poor; her sound value was on the order of £18,000 to £20,000 and her break-up value about half that. The owner had, a fortnight before placing the cover, refused a sale at about a third of the insured figure as a "breaker's price." The ship was insured at very nearly three times what the owner himself valued her at on a sale.

Second, as to the cargo. The cargo was insured by declaration at £41,000, of which £37,500 was a single parcel of machine tools. The declaration was increased to its full figure two days before sailing. The independent cargo surveyor did not see that parcel loaded, was directed away from the place it was said to be loaded, and certifies she cannot say any part of it came aboard. No charter, bill of lading, consignee, or freight for that parcel appears anywhere in the firm's papers. The most valuable item of the cargo, and the foundation of £37,500 of insurance, has no existence outside the manifest and the insurance declaration.

Third, as to the manner of the loss. The ship struck the Carrack Shoal lightly in fog and filled past her pumps within twenty minutes and foundered. Sound and ample pumps, by the chief engineer's account, made no impression. The salvage diver, on examining the wreck, found the hull bottom sound but for slight damage insufficient to sink her, the main sea inlet wide open and undamaged (opened, in his opinion, by a deliberate hand), and the bilge suction valves wired shut. The marks are those of a scuttling, not a stranding.

Fourth, as to the man below. The second engineer, engaged by the firm over the chief engineer's written objection and paid sums marked "special" with no service named, had the engine-room watch when she struck and was seen by two firemen to go down to the sea inlet and the bilge gear when the rest came up, and did not himself come up.

Fifth, as to the owner's papers. The firm's letter-book, produced under compulsion, contains the owner's correspondence placing the high hull cover, raising the cargo declaration, directing the master to leave the tools parcel to the firm and sign for it unseen, directing the surveyor be kept off the offshore side, and instructing the second engineer privately to see to the sea inlet and the bilge gear "low down and aft, and quickly, so that the pumps cannot be got on her," and to burn the letter.

I do not, in a preliminary memorandum, pronounce upon the cause; that is for a full inquiry and an assessor. I report only that these five matters, taken together, are wholly inconsistent with the honest accidental loss the preliminary inquiry found, and that the claim should not be paid until the matter is sifted to the bottom. I recommend the appointment of an assessor and the production of every record, hostile or not.

## Appendix XVI — Note on the order of the records

A practical note for the assessor. The five records the office requires before it will receive a finding (the salvage diver's report, the cargo surveyor's tally, the policy schedule, the owner's letters, and the cargo manifest) are the five that, between them, settle the four particulars of the finding: the manner of the loss, the place, the responsible party, and, with the timeline, the decisive minute. The diver's report settles the manner (scuttling, by the open sea inlet) and the place (the engine room). The tally and the manifest, read together, settle that the cargo was false. The policy schedule settles the motive. The owner's letters settle the arrangement and its author. No one of the five settles the case alone; each is dull and innocent apart; the case is in their conjunction. Draw all five, read them together, and the four particulars of the finding fall out of them. The other records fix the hours, corroborate, and exclude the alternatives, but the five are the spine of the finding, and the office requires them for that reason.

The owner's letter-book and the firm's private memoranda were produced ashore, under compulsion of the underwriters, the firm having declined to produce them voluntarily; the letter-book contains retained copies of the owner's correspondence, including the private letters to the second engineer marked to be burned, which survive as letter-book copies though the originals (if received and kept by Lund) went down with the ship.

## Appendix XVIII — The vessel and her service

The Marisol was an iron and steel single-screw cargo steamer of 1,840 tons gross and 1,120 tons net register, built in 1898 on the Clyde by Garrock and Sons to the order of a Newcastle owner, and sold twice before she came to Veil and Penhallow in the spring of 1921. Her principal dimensions were a length of 280 feet between perpendiculars, a moulded breadth of 38 feet, and a depth of 22 feet to the main deck. She was driven by a single triple-expansion engine of some eleven hundred indicated horse-power supplied by two single-ended boilers working at a hundred and eighty pounds, giving her a service speed of about nine knots in fair weather and rather less when deep. She had four holds, two forward and two aft of the engine and boiler space amidships, with a between-deck throughout, and was rigged with derricks at four hatches for the working of her own cargo.

She was, at the time of her loss, six and twenty years old, which for an iron-hulled tramp of her class is an age at which a vessel is sound enough for ordinary trades if she has been kept up but is no longer of any great value, her worth lying nearer her breaking-up price than her first cost. Her certificates of survey were in order and had been renewed at Liverpool before the voyage; nothing in the survey record suggests she was unfit for the passage she undertook, and nothing in the manner of her loss turns on her age or her condition, for an old ship as much as a new one floats until the sea is let into her.

Her trade in the three years of the present ownership had been the carriage of general cargo, coal outward and miscellaneous goods homeward, between the Mersey, the Bristol Channel, and the ports of Spain and Portugal, a plain and unremarkable employment in which she had made some forty voyages without serious mishap. The voyage on which she was lost was of the ordinary kind for her, Liverpool for Lisbon with a general cargo, and there was nothing in the orders for the voyage, so far as the master had them, to mark it from any other she had made.

## Appendix XIX — The ship's company

The Marisol carried on her last voyage a company of twenty-six, all told, of whom every one was saved and landed by the rescuing vessel, so that the loss, grave as it was to the underwriters, was happily no loss of life. The company is set out here for completeness, the officers and certificated men by name and the remainder by rating.

The master was Aldous Frane, holding an ordinary master's certificate of long standing. The chief officer, or first mate, was Daniel Okonkwo, certificated, who kept the deck log. The second officer was a Mr Harbenden, who had charge of the forenoon and first watches and gives an account in no way at variance with the mate's. The chief engineer was Tomas Such, certificated, and the second engineer was Edvard Lund, who had joined the ship for this voyage only. There were besides a boatswain, a carpenter, four able seamen, two ordinary seamen, six firemen and trimmers for the stokehold, a donkeyman, a cook, a steward, two boys, and a young wireless operator named Pris, who sent the one call that was sent.

The depositions of the company, beyond those of the master, the two officers, the chief and second engineers, and the firemen whose accounts bear on the engine room, are of the ordinary kind and add nothing to the question the inquiry must settle; they speak of the fog, the striking, the sounding of the wells, the order to the boats, and the good discipline with which the boats were got away and the people saved. They are filed with the survivors' accounts, and the assessor may read them, but the office does not require them, the matter turning on the five records named in file 08 and not on the general body of the crew's recollection of an orderly abandonment.

## Appendix XX — The Board's procedure in formal investigation

For the assistance of an assessor not familiar with the form of these proceedings, the standing procedure of a formal investigation into the loss of a ship is shortly this. The loss being reported, a preliminary inquiry is held, which gathers the depositions and the ship's papers and forms a preliminary view of the cause; this preliminary view is recorded but is not binding, and is frequently set aside by the formal investigation that follows. The formal investigation is conducted by an assessor who has before him the whole of the gathered material and who is charged to find, upon the evidence, the cause of the loss and the party, if any, in default.

It is to be borne in mind throughout that the preliminary view is the view of an inquiry that has not yet had the records read against one another, and that is shaped, more than it should be, by the order in which the papers are presented and by the account of those most ready to give one. The formal investigation exists precisely because the preliminary view is so often wrong, and the assessor is not to take the preliminary view as his starting point but to come to the records fresh and let them lead him where they lead. A finding that merely ratifies the preliminary view, without the records having been tried against it, is no finding at all in the sense the office requires.

The finding, when reached, is entered in the form set out in file 08, of four particulars, and is final. The office adjudicates it against its own sealed conclusion and returns it sound or unsound. The assessor should understand that the office does not return the answer, only whether the assessor has found it; the reasoning is the assessor's own, and the office looks only to whether the four particulars are right and the preconditions met.

## Appendix XXI — The wreck and the conditions of the survey

The Marisol lies on a steep edge of soft ground a little to seaward of the Carrack Shoal proper, in some fourteen fathoms at low water, upright and little broken, her decks fouled with weed and her holds open to the sea. The salvage survey was made on two descents in a slack of the tide, the set off the shoal being too strong for safe work except at the turn; the diver had good light on the upper works and lamp-light below, and reports the visibility in the water as fair for the season. The forward holds and the engine and boiler space were entered and examined; the after holds were not penetrated beyond the hatch coamings, the cargo and ballast having shifted against the doors.

Nothing in the conditions of the survey is in any doubt, and the diver's findings, twice descended for and reported with the particularity of a man who knew the underwriters would weigh every word, are to be taken as they stand. Where his report gives a measurement or describes the state of a fitting, that is the state in which he found it, and the inquiry has neither reason nor evidence to set against him.

## Appendix XXII — A short glossary

For the assistance of the assessor, a few of the terms used in the records are here explained. A sea-cock, or sea inlet valve, is a valve in the ship's bottom or low in her side by which sea water is admitted for the use of the engine and the pumps; opened, it admits the sea, and it is fitted low down for that purpose. The bilge suction valves are the valves on the engine-room manifold through which the bilge pumps draw the water that collects in the lowest part of the ship; secured shut, the pumps cannot draw, whatever their power. To scuttle a ship is to sink her deliberately by letting the sea into her, as distinct from a stranding, in which she is lost by taking the ground. Barratry, in the law of marine insurance, is a wrongful act wilfully committed by the master or crew to the prejudice of the owner; it is a term of the underwriters' art and does not itself appear among the four particulars the assessor enters. The load-line is the mark on a ship's side below which she may not be loaded; a ship floating above her marks is loaded more lightly than her draught of water would allow.

## Appendix XXIII — A note for the assessor on the use of this file

A word, finally, on how the present file is meant to be used, for it is larger than a loss of this kind usually generates and an assessor may reasonably ask why. The bulk of it is not padding. A marine loss of any difficulty is settled not by any single document but by the agreement, or the disagreement, of several, and the office has learned over many inquiries that the way to a wrong finding is to read the documents one at a time, take each at the face it presents, and stop at the first that offers a tidy account. The tidy account is very often the one the loss was arranged to present. The file is therefore set out at length, with the principal records reproduced in full in these appendices and not merely summarised, so that the assessor has before him the originals and can set each against the others and see where they part company.

The assessor will find, as he reads, that the file falls into two unequal parts. The greater part, in volume, is the ordinary paper of an ordinary loss: the deck log of a careful master, the weather of a foggy night, the wireless call, the boats got away in good order, the depositions of a shocked and honest crew, the surveyor's certificates all in order. Read in that spirit, and in the order the firm and the preliminary inquiry would have them read, these papers compose a picture of misfortune, and a finding drawn from them alone is a finding of misfortune. The smaller part, in volume, but the greater in weight, is a handful of records that do not fit that picture: a fitting found in a state no misfortune explains, a cargo certified absent that the manifest declares present, a schedule of insured sums out of all proportion to the things insured, a bundle of an owner's letters. These are the records the office requires, and they are required precisely because they are the ones the surface of the file would have the assessor pass over.

The discipline the office asks is simple to state and hard to keep. Do not settle the cause from the bulk of the file. Settle it from the agreement of the required records, read together and against the rest, and let the bulk of the file be tested against that finding rather than the finding be drawn from the bulk. An assessor who keeps that discipline will not be led by the volume of ordinary paper into the ordinary finding; he will be led by the few extraordinary records to the extraordinary one, if the records bear it. Whether they bear it is the work; this file does not do that work for the assessor, and is not meant to. It lays the records before him in full and leaves the weighing of them, which is the whole of the assessor's office, to him.

### A closing note from the assessor's file

The prior assessor left, clipped to the appendices, a single sheet that may serve to close this file as it opened it. It read:

"Everything the office requires to settle this case is in five records, and every one of the five is dull on its own. A diver's report of valve fittings. A surveyor's count of cargo. A schedule of figures. A manifest. A bundle of an owner's letters about insurance and engineers. Read singly, in the order the firm and the Board would have you read them, they are the ordinary paper of an ordinary loss, and they file as an accident. Read together, set one against another, they are not an accident at all. The sea inlet was opened by a hand. The valuable cargo was never aboard. The old ship and the phantom cargo were insured for a fortune. The owner's own engineer was paid and told, in writing, to open the sea low down and aft before the pumps could be got on her. And the owner was a hundred leagues away, hands clean, when his ship went down to his own profit. The file points at the master and the fog. The records point elsewhere, and the records are the assessor's master, not the file. Read the five together. Then enter your finding."
