# The Procedure of the Inquiry

This section sets out the procedure under which you will conduct your assessment and the form in which a finding may be entered. None of it is conjecture. It follows the practice of the underwriters' inquiry and the standing rules of the case office, and it has been confirmed by the assessor who opened the file before you and whose working notes are appended. The procedure does not vary.

## The standing of the inquiry

The Board of Trade's preliminary inquiry has reported, provisionally, a loss by stranding in fog, an honest accident, with such criticism as it offers directed at the navigation of the ship. That report is before you. It is not your finding and it does not bind you. The underwriters, dissatisfied, have opened this fuller inquiry and appointed you its assessor. Your task is to reach the true cause of the loss of the Marisol upon the whole of the evidence, and to enter a finding accordingly, whether or not that finding agrees with the Board.

You will find as you read that the file leans one way. The preliminary report leans that way; most of the depositions lean that way; the rumour current in the shipping houses of Liverpool leans that way. The records do not all lean that way. Your duty is to the records.

## How the records are worked

The case office holds the original records under seal, and releases them to the assessor for examination one by one upon request, recording each as it is drawn. You do not read the originals all at once; you draw them, examine them, and take into your working file a copy of each that you have examined. The records log (file 07) lists every record by its short reference of the form rec-something, with a note of what it is and where it is held. The appendices (file 09) carry the longer documents in full.

The procedure is this. You draw a record, you examine it, you copy its substance into your working file, and you go on to the next. Drawing and examining a record takes time, which the office notes against your inquiry. You may draw the same record more than once if a later reading throws new light on an earlier one; many of these records repay a second examination.

What you carry in your working file accumulates. The records themselves remain in the office under seal; what you accumulate is your examined copy of each, and it is upon the body of records you have examined and copied that your finding will be received and weighed. A finding entered without the decisive records in your file is a finding the office cannot receive, for the plain reason that you would not yet have looked at the matters on which the case turns.

## The requirement of a complete pass

You may not enter your finding upon a first and partial reading. The office will not receive it. You must have worked the file through at least once in full, drawing and examining the records, before a finding will be entertained.

This rule exists for the same reason that no marine court will hear a cause before the evidence is in. An assessor who has read three depositions and one log entry is in no position to say how a ship was lost. The rule obliges you to live with the whole file before you commit to a finding: to have read every deposition, examined every record on the list, and formed a considered view, rather than seizing on the first impression the surface of the file presents.

You will know you have completed a full pass when you have drawn and examined each record the office holds and copied its substance into your working file, and have returned to the beginning able to read the file as a whole. On a first pass, do not try to settle the case. Read the vessel and the voyage. Read the depositions. Draw the records in order. Get the shape of the thing into your head.

On a second pass, read for the contradictions. Set one deposition against another. Set a deposition against a record. Watch where a man's account of the night cannot be true if a record is true, and ask yourself which to believe.

On a third pass, you will be carrying in your file the records the case turns upon, you will have read each deposition two or three times, and you will be in a position to enter a finding. Do not hurry to it.

## The five records the finding rests upon

The office requires that certain records be in your working file before it will receive a finding. These are the records without which no finding could be sound, the records that fix the cause of the loss and the responsibility for it. They are named in the rules for entering the finding (file 08) and listed in the records log (file 07). Take the authoritative list from the office's own statement of it, not from any passing mention elsewhere in the file.

For the present inquiry the office requires, before it will receive a finding, that your working file contain the salvage diver's report, the cargo surveyor's tally, the policy schedule, the owner's letters, and the cargo manifest. Examine and copy all five before you enter anything. Other records bear on the case and reward examination, the deck log, the wireless log, the boiler report, the weather log, the several depositions, the load-line certificate, the survivors' accounts; but the five named are the ones the office regards as indispensable, and a finding entered without them in your file will be refused as premature, the assessor not yet having looked at the matters that decide the cause.

## The form of the finding

When you are ready, you enter a single finding to the case office. You must state four things.

The party responsible for the loss, named by the identifier given in the parties section (file 03), of the form par-something, drawn from the persons concerned.

The means by which the ship was lost, the mechanism of the loss, named by the office's short reference for it.

The place of the loss, where the thing that lost her was done, named by the office's reference.

The time at which the decisive act occurred, in hours and minutes on the 24-hour clock, in the form HH:MM, ship's time on the night of 14 November 1924.

You must also have in your working file, at the moment you enter the finding, the records the office requires (the five named above). If you enter a finding without those records examined and in your file, it will be refused on procedure and recorded against you as unsound.

A finding that names the right party, the right means, the right place, and the right time, with the required records in your file and a full pass completed, will be received and recorded as sound. A finding wrong in any one of those particulars will be received as unsound, with a note of which particular was wrong, and you may consider that note in framing a further finding.

## On the order of work

The assessor who opened this file left, on the first leaf of his notebook, a single line, underscored twice. It read: "Read the records before you read the rumour." The advice is sound. The rumour is loud and arrives first. The records are quiet and must be drawn one by one. The temptation, in a file that leans so plainly toward an accident and toward the master, is to enter that finding and be done. Resist it until you have set the salvage diver's report beside the deck log, and the surveyor's tally beside the manifest, and the policy schedule beside what the ship and her cargo were truly worth, and the owner's letters beside the whole. When you have done that, the finding will not be the one the file first pressed upon you, and you will know why.

## The common pitfalls of an inquiry of this kind

The assessor who goes wrong in a case like this one goes wrong, almost always, in one of a few well-worn ways. They are set out here so that you may know them and avoid them.

The first pitfall is to follow the file's own emphasis. The preliminary report is confident, the depositions mostly lean its way, and the rumour is loud and early. An assessor who weighs the evidence by how loudly the file presses it will find for an accident and against the master, because that is what the file presses. But the loudest evidence in a case of arranged loss is, of necessity, the evidence the arranger was content to have heard; the evidence that undoes him is quiet, because he could not make it loud without drawing attention to it. Weigh the records by what they establish, not by how the file ranks them.

The second pitfall is to take each suspicious fact alone and find it innocent. Any one of the decisive facts, taken by itself, can be explained away, and the file explains each away in turn: the high hull cover is the owner's entitlement to insure at value to him; the late cargo declaration is the ordinary act of a shipper whose parcel is made up; the surveyor's failure to see the tools is a matter of where she stood on the quay; the engineer below is a brave man at his post; the owner's letters are private correspondence wrenched from its setting. Each explanation is plausible in isolation. The case is not in any fact alone; it is in the conjunction of all of them, which no single innocent explanation reaches, and which the file is careful never to assemble in one place. Assemble them.

The third pitfall is to fix on the hand and miss the head. The man who opened the sea to the ship is below, drowned, and it is tempting to lay the loss at his door and be done. But a second engineer cannot insure a ship he does not own, cannot declare a cargo he does not control, and cannot gain by a loss that pays him only his wage. The hand that does an act at another's instance is not the author of it. Ask always, of the man below, at whose instance and for whose gain he acted; the answer is not in the engine room.

The fourth pitfall is to confuse the place the ship touched with the place she was lost, and the minute she struck with the minute she was doomed. She touched the Carrack Shoal; she was lost in her own engine room, by the sea inlet. She struck at 23:10; she was doomed at about 23:15, when the sea was let into her, and abandoned at 23:40, when the fact was plain to the bridge. An assessor who names the shoal as the place, or the striking as the decisive minute, has named the accident and not the act. The act has its own place and its own minute, and they are not the ship's.

Avoid these four, and the records will carry you to the right finding. Fall into any of them, and you will file the loss as the arranger meant it to be filed.

## A word from the assessor who went before

The notes of the prior assessor contain a passage that may steady a newcomer to this kind of work.

"On a first reading of a marine casualty the assessor is overwhelmed. There are a hundred records and twenty men and every one of them has an account of the night, and the accounts agree just enough to seem solid and disagree just enough to seem honest. The instinct is to find the loudest explanation and adopt it. In this file the loudest explanation is fog, and behind the fog a tired and careless master, and it is an explanation that asks nothing further of you. Do not adopt it because it is loud. A fog explains why a ship cannot see the shoal. It does not explain why she filled in minutes when her pumps were built to give her hours, and it does not explain what was, and was not, in her holds, and it does not explain what she was insured for, and it does not explain a sea-cock found open in deep water by a diver who had no reason to invent it. Hold every one of those quiet facts in your hand at once. The man who lost the Marisol is in this file. He is not the man the file points to. Find him by the records, and the records alone."

Read the records. Reason slowly. Enter your finding only when the evidence has made it sure.
