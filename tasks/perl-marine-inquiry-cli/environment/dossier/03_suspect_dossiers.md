# The Parties

Seven persons recur throughout the file of the Marisol. What follows is a working dossier on each, drawn from the ship's articles, from the firm's papers, from the depositions, from the underwriters' correspondence, and from the impressions formed at the Board of Trade's preliminary inquiry. Each party carries an identifier of the form par-something. That identifier is the form in which any finding against the party is to be entered.

These dossiers are working documents. They are not impartial. They carry, in places, the leanings of the people who supplied the information, and in particular they carry the leaning of the preliminary inquiry, which had formed a view before the underwriters opened this fuller one. Treat them as a starting point, not as a finding.

## par-frane — Captain Aldous Frane, master

Master of the Marisol on her last voyage, and her master these four years past. A Liverpool man, fifty-one years of age, holding a master's certificate of long standing, with an unremarkable record at sea and no casualty against his name before this one. He brought the Marisol onto the Carrack Shoal in fog on the night of 14 November and, when she began to fill, gave the order to abandon. He was taken up in the starboard boat and landed at Oporto, and he gave his evidence to the preliminary inquiry frankly and without reserve.

It is upon Captain Frane that the preliminary inquiry's criticism falls, and it is his name that the rumour in the shipping houses attaches to the loss. The criticism is of two kinds. The first is that he navigated negligently: that he stood on too long in thickening fog, that he did not sound, that he did not reduce to a dead slow bell in time, and that by these omissions he set his ship on a shoal that lay well off the proper track. The second is that he abandoned too soon: that he gave the order to take to the boats while the ship might yet have been saved, and that the five lives lost in the launching were lost needlessly.

You will read Captain Frane's own account of the run-in and of the abandonment in the depositions, and you should read it carefully and more than once, for it is fuller and steadier than the rumour allows. Mark in particular what he says of the rate at which the water gained on him, and ask yourself whether a master who found his ship filling at that rate, with his pumps making no impression, had any honest choice but to get his people away. A master is criticised for abandoning too soon only by those who do not know why she was filling. Captain Frane did not know why she was filling. He believed, as any master would, that he had struck hard on the shoal and opened her up, and he acted upon that belief. Whether that belief was true is the question this inquiry exists to settle.

He had no interest of his own in the loss. He owned no part of the ship. He carried no insurance on her or her cargo. He had no debts that her loss would discharge and no expectation from it. He stood, on the contrary, to lose his certificate and his livelihood by it, as he very nearly has. Keep that by you when you weigh his account against the cheaper explanation.

A word on the two charges against him, for they are the whole of the case against the master and they will not bear much weight. The charge of negligent navigation rests on his coming onto a shoal that lay off his track; but he reduced speed, posted lookouts, sounded, and got no bottom where he was, and his account of being set in by an unseen inset current is a known and sufficient explanation of how a careful man comes onto foul ground in fog (see the navigation note, file 09). The charge of abandoning too soon rests on the assumption that the ship might have been saved; but she was filling past sound pumps from a cause the master could not see, and a master who keeps his people aboard a ship filling past her pumps in fog on a falling tide drowns them. Both charges assume an honest grounding. Both dissolve the moment the cause of the filling is known to be other than a grounding. The master is the scapegoat of a file that did not look below the waterline; he is not the author of the loss, and on a careful reading he is not even negligent.

## par-veil — Mr Marcus Veil, managing owner

Managing owner of the Marisol and senior partner of Veil and Penhallow, ship managers and agents, of Liverpool. He was ashore in Liverpool throughout the voyage and never set foot aboard the Marisol for it. He gave evidence to the preliminary inquiry, briefly, as the owner naturally would, and his evidence was directed to the value of the ship and the cargo, to the insurances, and to his entire confidence in his master, of whom he spoke handsomely.

Mr Veil is, on the face of the file, a witness and not a party to anything. He was a hundred leagues away when his ship struck. He is the man who lost a ship and stands to be paid for it, which is the ordinary and innocent position of every shipowner whose vessel is wrecked. The preliminary inquiry treated him so, and asked him little beyond the figures.

Certain plain facts of his position are recorded in the file, scattered among the records: that he owned the whole of the ship through a single-ship company he controlled; that the insurances on her were placed through his firm; that the declaration of her cargo, the machine tools included, was made or approved by him; and that, upon a total loss, the insurance money on hull and cargo would come to him or to his order. These facts are nowhere gathered together. They lie apart, in the policy schedule, in the surveyor's tally, in his own correspondence, and in what an old steamer and an ordinary cargo were truly worth. The preliminary inquiry, looking only at the navigation, had no occasion to set them side by side, and the file as it stands does not set them side by side for you.

## par-lund — Mr Edvard Lund, second engineer

Second engineer of the Marisol, lost in the casualty. A Norwegian by birth, naturalised, in his late thirties, engaged for this voyage and the two before it. He was below in the engine room through the first watch on the night of the loss. He was last seen by a surviving fireman going further below, toward the engine-room plates and the after end, in the minutes after she struck, at a time when every other engine-room hand was making for the ladders and the deck. His body was not recovered. He is presumed lost with the ship.

Mr Lund had been engaged by the firm, that is to say by Mr Veil, and not in the ordinary way through the pool. The chief engineer remarks, in his deposition, that Lund was the owner's man rather than his own, put aboard over the chief's preference, and kept to himself. You will read what the surviving fireman saw of his movements below, and where he was when he should have been at the ladders, and you will form a view of what a second engineer was doing going down toward the sea-cock and the bilge manifold when the ship was filling and every instinct of self-preservation was to go up.

Whatever Lund did below, he did as a hired hand. He had no insurable interest in the Marisol. He held no policy and placed none, so he could not have over-insured her. He had no part in the declaration of the cargo and was not present when the lighters were tallied, so he could not have falsified the lading. A second engineer cannot arrange the over-insurance of a ship and the lading of a fictitious cargo from the engine room; those are an owner's acts, not an engineer's. If Lund opened the sea to her, the question that follows is at whose instance and for whose gain, for it plainly served no purpose of his own; a man does not sink the ship he stands aboard of for his wages. The instrument serves an interest. Whose interest the loss served is a matter for the policy schedule and the owner's papers, not for the engine room.

## par-okonkwo — Mr Daniel Okonkwo, first mate

First mate of the Marisol, taken up in the starboard boat with the master. A West African by birth, a British subject, an experienced officer holding a mate's certificate, four voyages in the ship. He had the deck for part of the run-in on the evening of the 14th and was on the bridge with the master when she struck. He gave a full and careful deposition.

Mr Okonkwo is a witness, not a party. He had no interest in the loss and stood only to lose by it, as the master did. His value to the inquiry is the value of an honest and observant officer who was on the bridge through the critical hour and at the holds during the loading at Liverpool. Read his deposition with attention to two matters: what he says of the loading, of the holds he saw worked and the holds he did not, and of the lighters that came alongside and the lighters that did not; and what he says of the rate at which the ship filled after she struck, which an officer of his standing was well able to judge and which does not agree with an honest grounding. He does not draw the inference. He gives you the facts from which the inference is to be drawn, and he gives them plainly, in the middle of a long deposition, where they are easily passed over.

## par-such — Mr Tomas Such, chief engineer

Chief engineer of the Marisol, taken up in the port boat. A Liverpool man of long service, twenty years at sea, holding a first-class certificate. He was off watch and asleep in his bunk when the ship struck, the second engineer having the engine room. He was roused by the striking, came below, found the water already gaining and the engine room filling, and got his people away.

Mr Such is a witness, not a party. He was asleep when the decisive act was done and had no hand in it, and he had no interest in the loss. His value is twofold. First, he can speak to the condition and the pumping power of the ship, which he knew better than any man, and he is clear that the pumps were sound and ample and that no mere grounding should have outrun them. Second, he can speak to the second engineer, Lund, whom he distrusted as the owner's placement, and to the state in which he found the engine room when he reached it, the sea-cock, the bilge manifold, the valves. Read what he found, and what he could not understand about it, and remember that he is describing the very fittings the salvage diver later found in the same condition on the bottom.

## par-cardew — Miss Hester Cardew, cargo surveyor

An independent cargo surveyor, engaged not by the firm but by a sub-underwriter on the cargo, to attend the loading at Liverpool and tally the goods actually put aboard against the manifest. A woman of about forty, of some years' standing in the trade, careful and exact in her methods, with no interest in the ship or the firm and a professional interest only in the truth of her tally. She was at the quay through the loading of the 11th and 12th of November.

Miss Cardew is a witness, and the most quietly decisive witness in the file, though her tally was filed, read once, and set aside by the preliminary inquiry as a matter of cargo bookkeeping with no bearing on a navigational loss. Her tally records what came over the side and into the holds, lighter by lighter, lift by lift. Read it against the manifest. The manifest declares a consignment of machine tools of great value, stowed in numbers two and three holds. Her tally records what was actually struck below. Where the two part company is the heart of the case, and she states the parting plainly, without comment, as a surveyor states a count. She does not say that the cargo was fictitious. She gives you the count from which you must conclude it.

## par-mercer — Mr Mercer, underwriter's agent

The underwriter's agent who placed the insurances on the Marisol and her cargo and who holds the policy schedules. A broker of the Liverpool market, acting for the underwriters, dealing with the firm of Veil and Penhallow in the placing of the risks. He gave evidence to the present inquiry, not to the preliminary one, the underwriters having called him when they opened it.

Mr Mercer is a witness, not a party. His value is the policy schedule, which he produces and explains: the sum insured on the hull, the sum insured on the cargo, the dates the cover was placed and increased, and the relation of those sums to the real value of an old ship and to the declared value of the cargo. Read what he says of when the cargo cover was increased and at whose instance, and set the sums he gives against the breaker's value of a twenty-six-year-old steamer and against the surveyor's tally of what was truly in the holds. He does not allege a fraud. He gives you the figures by which a fraud is measured.

## The parties weighed as candidates for principal

It is worth setting out, before the depositions, what the loss requires of whoever arranged it, and which of the seven could have done those things. The assessor should test each party against the requirements and see who survives the testing; the parties file does not name the survivor, but it sets out the test.

Whoever arranged the loss of the Marisol had to be able to do four things. He had to have an interest in her loss, that is, to gain by it; for no one sinks a ship he does not profit by sinking. He had to be able to over-insure her, to carry an old ship and a doubtful cargo at sums far above their worth, which only the person who valued her and placed her insurances could do. He had to be able to falsify her cargo, to declare a valuable parcel that was never aboard, which only the person who made or approved the cargo declaration and controlled the loading could do. And he had to be able to procure the act below, to have aboard a man who would open the sea to her, which required the engaging and the paying and the instructing of that man. Hold those four requirements in mind and try each party against them.

The master, Frane, fails the first at once: he had no interest in the loss and stood only to lose by it, and he could neither over-insure the ship nor declare her cargo nor engage her engineers, none of which is a master's office. He is a candidate for negligence, on the file's reading, but not for the arranging of a loss; he had neither the motive nor the means.

The chief engineer, Such, and the first mate, Okonkwo, fail likewise: neither had an interest in the loss, neither could insure or declare or engage, and both stood to lose by the casualty as the master did. They are witnesses to it, not authors of it; and Such was asleep when the act was done.

The surveyor, Cardew, and the broker, Mercer, are outside the ship's company altogether: the one counted the cargo for a sub-underwriter, the other placed the risks for the underwriters, and neither had any interest in the loss or any power to arrange it; their value is as the witnesses whose records measure the falsity of the cargo and the over-insurance.

The second engineer, Lund, meets the fourth requirement and no other. He was the man below who could open the sea; but he had no interest in the loss beyond his pay, no power to over-insure a ship he did not own, and no part in the declaration of a cargo he did not load. A hand that does the act at another's instance is not the principal; the principal is the one who supplied the interest, the over-insurance, and the false declaration, and who engaged and instructed the hand. Lund is the instrument the test reveals; he is not the arranger the test is looking for.

That leaves one party who meets all four requirements at once: who had the interest in the loss, who could and did over-insure the ship and her cargo, who made or approved the false cargo declaration, and who engaged, paid, and instructed the man below. The assessor should satisfy himself, from the records, that one and only one of the seven meets the whole of the test, and that the others fall at one requirement or another. The test is not offered here as a verdict. It is offered as the frame within which the records are to be read.

## A note on the parties, before you read further

Six of these seven persons are, on the face of it, witnesses, and only the master stands accused. As you read the depositions and draw the records, it is worth keeping in mind the questions the preliminary inquiry, fixed upon the navigation, did not put: not only who was on the bridge when she struck, but who gained by her loss, who was in a position to arrange the matters that bore upon it, and whose movements below in the engine room are unaccounted for at the critical time. The file does not answer those questions for you. It contains the materials of the answer, set down plainly and scattered wide, and the assembling of them is your work.
