# Owner's Correspondence and Private Papers

What follows are extracts from the private papers and correspondence of the firm of Veil and Penhallow, so far as they were produced to the present inquiry. Some are letters in Mr Veil's own hand or over his signature; some are entries from the firm's letter-book and the firm's private cash and policy memoranda; one is a fragment of a telegram. Where the original is a copy retained in the firm's letter-book, it is so marked. Where handwriting is doubtful, the doubtful word is given in square brackets.

A caution before you read. These papers were produced by the firm under compulsion of the underwriters and are, on their face, the ordinary correspondence of a ship management business: instructions to a master, dealings with a broker, declarations of cargo, engagements of officers. The preliminary inquiry, which saw most of them, read them as ordinary business and returned them without remark, and on a quick reading they are ordinary business. The matter is in the dates, in the sequence, and in what one letter says when it is set against another and against the records in file 07 and file 09. Read them slowly and in order. Read them with the policy schedule and the surveyor's tally beside you. The firm did not produce a letter that says, in terms, what was intended. It produced letters that, taken together and in their order, leave the intention in very little doubt.

The papers fall into three groups: the firm's dealings touching the insurances; the firm's dealings touching the cargo; and the firm's dealings with the second engineer, Lund. They are given here in that order, though in the file they came mixed, and you should note the dates as you go.

---

## Group one: the insurances

### Letter, Veil to Mercer, 9 October 1924 (letter-book copy)

Dear Mercer,

I am turning over in my mind the cover on the Marisol. She is an old ship, as you know, and the market for her kind is as bad as I have seen it; were I to sell her tomorrow I should be lucky to get a third of what I once gave, and the breakers would give me less. But she is worth more to me running than her market price, for I have the South Atlantic trade built round her and could not replace her under three times her break-up value. I should like to carry her at her value to me as a going concern, not at her scrap price. I am advised an owner may do so. Let me know what you can place, and on what terms, and do not let us be shy about the figure.

Yours, M.V.

### Memorandum, firm's policy book, 14 October 1924

Hull and machinery, SS Marisol, cover placed through Mercer at [figure entered in the schedule, file 09]. Note: this is well above present market and far above break-up. Premium correspondingly. M.V. instructs the higher valuation to stand.

### Letter, Veil to Mercer, 5 November 1924 (letter-book copy)

Dear Mercer,

The Marisol loads next week for Lisbon. The cargo will be a fuller and richer one than her ordinary run, the principal item being a large consignment of machine tools which I have engaged to carry for a Lisbon house at a good rate. The tools are of considerable value and I shall be declaring the cargo high on this voyage. I will send you the figure when the parcel is made up, which will be a day or two before she sails. See that the open policy will answer for the full declared value; I do not wish to be caught short on cover with a valuable parcel below.

Yours, M.V.

### Telegram fragment, Veil to Mercer, 11 November 1924

DECLARE CARGO MARISOL THIS VOYAGE AT [figure, see schedule file 09] STOP TOOLS MADE UP STOP SEE COVER ANSWERS FULL FIGURE BEFORE SAILING — VEIL

### Note, firm's policy book, 11 November 1924

Cargo declaration this voyage increased to full figure per M.V.'s wire, the tools parcel being made up. Cover confirmed by Mercer to answer. Note: declaration raised to its full figure two days before sailing. Whole of the declared cargo value is in the tools parcel; the cork, plate, and sundries are a small matter beside it.

Set these four papers in their order and observe only the plain facts of them: an old ship the owner himself describes as worth a third of her cover, insured high; and a cargo whose whole declared value lay in a single parcel of tools, the cover on which was run up to its full figure two days before the ship sailed. What those facts weigh is for the assessor to settle, with the surveyor's tally and the policy schedule beside them.

## Group two: the cargo

### Letter, Veil to the master of the Marisol (Frane), 7 November 1924 (letter-book copy)

Captain Frane,

The Marisol loads this week. Your mate will work the general cargo at the quay in the ordinary way. The consignment of machine tools, which is a particular parcel and a valuable one, the firm will see to itself: it is to be struck in from the offshore side by lighters the firm is sending, and stowed in numbers two and three by men the firm will put aboard for the purpose, so that you and your officers need give it no attention. Sign for the parcel as made up and stowed on the firm's certificate, which you will have. Make your usual passage for Lisbon. I have entire confidence in you.

Yours, M.V.

### Letter, Veil to the firm's quay agent, 7 November 1924 (letter-book copy)

See that the surveyor Cardew, who attends for the cargo underwriter, keeps to the quay hatch and is not at the offshore side when our lighters work the tools parcel. Tell her the firm certifies that parcel itself and she need not trouble. Keep her tally to what comes over the quayside. The firm's certificate will answer for the tools.

Set these two letters beside the surveyor's deposition and her tally (file 04, file 09). The master is told to leave the valuable parcel wholly to the firm and to sign for it on the firm's word. The independent surveyor is to be kept away from the only side on which that parcel could go aboard, and fobbed off with the firm's own certificate. And the surveyor's tally records that the parcel never passed over the quay in her sight. A cargo whose loading was hidden from the one independent witness, and certified only by the man who would be paid for its loss, is a cargo a careful assessor will want proof of before he takes it to have been aboard at all. Whether such proof exists anywhere in this file is a matter the assessor should satisfy himself upon.

### Note, firm's freight memoranda, undated, among the cargo papers

The Lisbon house: see no freight received from any Lisbon house for a tools parcel this voyage. No charter, no bill of lading lodged by a Lisbon consignee, no correspondence with a Lisbon buyer in the file. The tools were declared as carried for a Lisbon house at a good rate; there is no Lisbon house in the firm's papers.

This note, made by the underwriters' clerk who went through the cargo papers, is short and easily passed over, but it is worth its weight. A real consignment of machine tools carried for a Lisbon house at a good rate leaves a trail: a charter, a bill of lading, a consignee, freight money. There is none. The tools exist on the manifest and on the insurance declaration and nowhere else in the firm's own papers.

## Group three: the second engineer

### Letter, Veil to Lund, 6 November 1924 (letter-book copy, marked "private")

Lund,

You will sign on the Marisol again for this voyage to Lisbon, as second, under Such. I have spoken to you of what I require and I will not put more of it on paper than I must. You will keep the engine-room watch through the passage down the Portuguese coast and you will see that all is ready below as we discussed, the main injection and the bilge gear in particular, so that when the time comes there is no delay and no doubt. You understand the trim she is to sail in and why. Do your part as we agreed and you will have the remainder of what I promised you, and a berth as long as you want one. Say nothing to Such, who is not in this and need not be.

M.V.

### Note, firm's private cash book, 8 November 1924

Paid E. Lund, on account, [sum]. Marked "Marisol — special." A further like sum to follow "on completion." No service is named against the entry. The sum is several times an engineer's wage for the voyage.

### Letter, Veil to Lund, 10 November 1924 (letter-book copy, marked "private")

Lund,

The cargo is in hand and the cover is being seen to. The passage is straightforward. You know the place off the coast where the ground is foul and the fog lies thick at this season; you have run it with me on the chart. Once she is there and the watch is yours, the rest is as we settled, low down and aft, and quickly, so that the pumps cannot be got on her before she is past holding. Leave nothing that can be put right by the time the chief is below. After, look to yourself; the boats are your affair. Burn this.

M.V.

(The letter-book copy survives, though Lund's original, if he received and kept it, went down with him. The firm produced the copy under compulsion, with the others. The instruction is given in the language of a man who will not name the act. What "low down and aft," and "before the pumps can be got on her," and the matter of the trim refer to, the engineering evidence and the salvage diver's report in file 07 and file 09 will show; the assessor should read this letter with those before him and judge for himself what was required of the second engineer and by whom.)

## Group four: miscellaneous papers among those produced

These last items did not fall cleanly into the three groups above. They are produced here for completeness, and one or two of them are worth more than their plainness suggests.

### Letter, Veil to a Liverpool ship-broker, 2 October 1924 (letter-book copy)

Dear Sir, I have your note as to the Marisol. I am not disposed to sell her at the figure you name, which is a breaker's figure and an insult to a ship that has years in her yet for the right owner. I would sooner keep her in my own trade than give her away. (The inquiry notes: a fortnight before placing the high hull cover, the owner was offered, and refused as derisory, a sale price the assessor may compare with the figure he then insured her for. The figure the broker named, and which the owner called a breaker's figure, is in the inquiry's file at about a third of the sum insured.)

### Note, firm's office diary, 4 November 1924

Mr V. to the offices of the cargo underwriter re the open policy and the coming declaration. Engaged thereafter with the quay arrangements for the Marisol's loading. Capt. Frane to the office for sailing orders. Lund to see Mr V. privately; no note of the matter.

### Letter, the chief engineer Such to the firm, 28 October 1924 (received, in the firm's file)

Sirs, I am to take the Marisol again and am content, but I would urge that I be allowed to name my own second this voyage, having a good man available, rather than carry Mr Lund again, with whom I do not find the engine room runs as I would wish, he keeping much to himself and to the owner's instructions rather than to mine. (The inquiry notes: the chief engineer asked, in writing, to be rid of Lund and to name his own second, and was refused; Lund was carried again at the owner's insistence. The chief's instinct, recorded here before the loss and before any theory of it, that Lund answered to the owner and not to him, is borne out by the private letters in group three.)

### Telegram, Veil to the quay agent Tunley, 11 November 1924

KEEP CARDEW TO QUAY HATCH STOP OUR LIGHTERS WORK TOOLS OFFSHORE SIDE STOP FIRM CERTIFIES THAT PARCEL — VEIL

(The inquiry notes: this telegram, set beside the surveyor's tally and the quay agent's deposition, is the instruction by which the one independent witness to the loading was kept from the one parcel that mattered.)

## A note on the correspondence

You have now the firm's own papers, so far as the firm produced them. No one of them is a confession. The owner was at pains to put nothing on paper that names the act, and to a quick reader, or to the preliminary inquiry that returned these papers without remark, they are the ordinary correspondence of a man insuring his ship, declaring his cargo, instructing his master, and engaging his engineer.

But the matter of these papers is in their dates and their sequence, and that the assessor must work out for himself. Note when the hull cover was placed and at what valuation against an old ship the owner himself calls worth a third of it. Note when the cargo declaration was raised, and on what parcel, and whether any charter, consignee, or freight for that parcel appears anywhere else in the firm's own papers. Note what the master was told to do, and not to do, about that parcel. Note what was paid to the second engineer, on what account, for what named service, and what he was told in a private letter to see to below, and when, and that he was told to burn it.

The papers do not draw a conclusion, and none is offered here. They were not meant to be read together at all; the firm produced them severally, under compulsion, and trusted to their dullness apart. Whether, read together and against the records in file 07 and file 09, they amount to no more than the ordinary business of a ship management firm, or to something else, is precisely the question put to this inquiry, and the answer is to be reasoned from them and the records, not taken from any gloss upon them. Read them again, in order, before you decide.
