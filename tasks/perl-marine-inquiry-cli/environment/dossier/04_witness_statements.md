# Depositions

What follows are the depositions taken from the surviving officers and crew of the Marisol, and from certain witnesses ashore, in the course of the Board of Trade's preliminary inquiry at Liverpool in December 1924 and of the underwriters' fuller inquiry that followed. The earlier depositions were taken on oath before the preliminary inquiry; the later ones, marked as such, were taken before the present inquiry. They are reproduced here essentially as given, with only such smoothing as removes repetition and the disorder of speech.

The order is not chronological and not by rank. It is the order in which the depositions came into the file. Read them with the vessel-and-voyage section and the records log at hand, and read the longer ones, the master's, the first mate's, the chief engineer's, twice, for they carry, buried in a great deal of ordinary matter, the few facts on which the whole case turns.

A word of caution before you begin. Most of these men believe the loss was an accident, and they say so, honestly, because it is what they saw. A ship in fog, a striking, a filling, an abandonment: that is the night as it appeared from the deck and the boats, and a man who saw only that will tell you, in good faith, that the ship was lost by stranding in fog. The depositions therefore lean, almost all of them, the way the preliminary inquiry leaned. The few facts that do not fit that account are not flagged by the men who give them, because the men who give them did not know their importance. You must find them yourself.

## Deposition of Captain Aldous Frane, master (par-frane)

Taken before the preliminary inquiry, and supplemented before the present inquiry.

I am Aldous Frane, master mariner, holding a master's certificate these twenty-six years. I have been master of the Marisol four years. I joined her at Liverpool for the voyage in question on the 11th of November and we cleared on the forenoon of the 12th, bound for Lisbon with a general cargo.

The first two days were without event. The weather was moderate, the glass steady, and we made our ordinary passage south. I have made this passage many times. I know the coast and I know the set of the tides off the Portuguese approaches.

On the afternoon of the 14th the weather thickened. By the first dog watch we had fog, patchy at first and then settling thick, the thickest I have known on that coast. I reduced from full to half at about eight in the evening and posted extra lookouts forward and on the bridge wings. I had the lead got ready. We sounded twice in the evening and got no bottom at the depths we sounded, which was consistent with our reckoning that we were well to seaward of the foul ground.

I want to be clear upon this point, because I understand it has been said against me. We were, by my reckoning and by Mr Okonkwo's, well outside the Carrack Shoal and on the proper track for the Tagus. We had no expectation of shoal water where we were. The sounding we got, or rather the no-bottom we got, agreed with that. There is a strong inset current on that coast in certain conditions of wind and tide, and I have since concluded that we were set in toward the land by that current more than the reckoning allowed, in the fog, without our knowing it. That is the only way I can account for our being where we were. It is a thing that has happened to better navigators than I am, on that coast, in fog.

We struck at about ten minutes past eleven. It was not a heavy striking. I have been aground before and I know the feel of it, the long grinding check. This was a single shock and then she was fast forward, by the feel of it on the forefoot and the turn of the bilge, not driven hard up but held. I stopped the engine at once and rang down to stand by. I expected, I will be honest, that we should lie there till the tide made and float off, perhaps with some damage forward, and I gave my first orders on that expectation: sound round the ship, get the carpenter to the wells, find out what she had started.

What I did not expect, and what I cannot to this day account for by the striking I felt, is the rate at which she began to fill. Within a very few minutes of striking, the carpenter reported water gaining in number three hold and the engine room, and gaining fast. I rang down to the engine room and could get no clear answer at first. The chief came up to me himself a little later and said the engine room was filling faster than the pumps could hold and that he could not understand it, for the pumps were sound and he had them all on her. I have known Mr Such twenty years. When a man like that tells you he cannot understand why his pumps are making no impression, you believe him, and you do not waste the minutes he is buying you.

I gave the order to abandon at about twenty minutes before midnight. I gave it because the water was over the engine-room plates and rising, because the pumps were beaten, and because I judged, on the chief's report and my own observation, that she would not float and that to keep my people aboard a sinking ship in fog on a falling tide was to drown them. I will say plainly: had I believed she could be saved, I would not have abandoned her, and no man who knows the sea would have done. I did not abandon a ship that could be saved. I abandoned a ship that was filling past all saving, for a reason I did not then understand and which I leave to this inquiry to understand for me.

We got two boats away. The after boat fouled in the launching and was lost, and five men with her, and I will carry that to my grave whatever this inquiry finds. We were taken up the next forenoon by the coaster Estremoz and landed at Oporto.

Asked about the second engineer, Lund: he was the owner's man, engaged by the firm and put aboard. I had little to do with him. He kept the engine-room watch on the night in question, the chief being below in his bunk. I rang down to him after we struck and got no clear answer, as I have said. I did not see him again. I am told he was last seen going down into the engine room when others were coming up. I cannot speak to that. I was on the bridge.

Asked whether I sounded enough, whether I reduced soon enough, whether I should have stopped altogether in such fog: I have asked myself these questions every night since. I believe I navigated as a careful master navigates in fog on a coast he knows, and I believe the inset current set me in beyond what any reckoning would have shown. But I am not the one who can tell you why she filled as she filled, and until that is known, I say that the loss of the Marisol cannot be laid to my navigation alone, for no ship I ever heard of fills in twenty minutes from taking the ground as gently as she took it.

(Before the present inquiry, asked further about the fill rate.) I have thought of nothing else. A ship that takes the ground as she did, and starts a plate or springs a seam, makes water, yes, but at a rate a sound pump holds. We had two sound pumps and the power to drive them. For the water to beat them, the sea must have been coming in by a bore far larger than any seam that gentle a striking would open. Where such a bore was, and how it came to be open, I did not know that night and do not know now of my own knowledge. I only know it was there, by its effect.

## Deposition of Mr Daniel Okonkwo, first mate (par-okonkwo)

Taken before the preliminary inquiry, and supplemented before the present inquiry.

I am Daniel Okonkwo, first mate of the Marisol, holding a mate's certificate. I have served four voyages in her. I was on the bridge with the master when she struck and I was in his boat when we abandoned.

I will speak first of the loading at Liverpool, because I was the officer who had charge of the holds during the loading, under the master, and I was asked little about it before and I think it matters.

We loaded at the north quay on the 11th and finished on the forenoon of the 12th. The cargo was a general one. By the manifest the principal item, and far the most valuable, was a consignment of machine tools, a large parcel of it, to be stowed in numbers two and three holds, the heavy lifts. There was besides a quantity of cork, and tin plate, and sundries, and some empty drums and tins going back south, for number one hold mostly.

Now I will tell you what I saw worked, because I had the deck for the loading and I tallied the lighters that came alongside, in a rough way, as a mate does. The cork came aboard, and the tin plate, and the sundries, and they went into number one and the forward part of number two. The empties came aboard. But the great parcel of machine tools, the heavy lifts that were to fill numbers two and three and that the manifest made so much of, I did not see worked. We rigged the heavy derricks for them, at the firm's instruction, and stood by for the lighters, and the lighters that were to bring them did not come, or came light. I remember being told by the firm's man on the quay that the tools were being struck in from the offshore side by lighter, to save the quay cranes, and would be aboard before we sailed, and that I was not to concern myself, the firm would see to the stowage of that parcel itself. And number three hold, when we battened down, was not full as a hold full of machine tools should be full. It was down by the head a little on departure, and we trimmed her with water ballast in the after peak to bring her right, which a mate does not do for a ship properly laden aft with a heavy cargo.

I thought little of it at the time. The firm's man said the tools were aboard and stowed and I had the firm's word for it, and a mate does not call the owner a liar over his own cargo. But I will say to this inquiry what I would not say to the firm: I did not, with my own eyes, see the machine tools struck below, and the trim of the ship on departure was not the trim of a ship with that weight low in her after holds. What was in numbers two and three when we sailed, beyond the cork and the sundries forward, I cannot of my own knowledge say. There was ballast in her that should not have been needed had the holds been full.

I will speak now of the night. I had the first watch with the master. The fog was very thick. We sounded and got no bottom. We were, by every reckoning I could make, well outside the shoal. When she struck it was a light striking, not the striking of a ship driven hard aground. I felt her check forward and then she was held, but gently.

The water came up very fast after that. I have been in a stranded ship before and I know how a ship makes water when she has taken the ground and started something forward. This was not that. This was a ship filling from low down and from aft, from the engine room and number three, faster than anything a started plate forward would account for. I said as much to the master at the time. The chief came up and said his pumps could not hold it and he could not understand why. I understood the master's order to abandon and I think any officer would have given it. She was going, and going faster than she had any right to from the blow she took.

Asked whether the master navigated negligently: I do not think so. We sounded, we reduced, we kept our lookouts. The fog was the worst I have seen and the current set us in. I would not have done other than the master did, on the bridge that night, and I was on the bridge.

Asked about the second engineer, Lund: I knew him little. He was the owner's placement. On the night, I am told, he stayed below when the engine room was being abandoned. I did not see it. A fireman who came up in my boat spoke of it. You should ask the firemen.

## Deposition of Mr Tomas Such, chief engineer (par-such)

Taken before the preliminary inquiry, and supplemented before the present inquiry.

I am Tomas Such, chief engineer of the Marisol, first-class certificate, twenty years at sea, three voyages in this ship. I was off watch and asleep in my bunk when she struck. The second engineer, Lund, had the engine-room watch.

I will speak to the pumps first, because it is the thing I know best and the thing that has troubled me most. The Marisol had two bilge pumps able to draw on the bilge main: the main pump off the engine, and an independent steam pump, a Worthington, in the engine room. Both were in good order. I had them surveyed and I had worked them and I knew them. Between them they had ample power to hold down any leak that a grounding of the kind we suffered would open. I say that as the man who knew those pumps. A started plate, a sprung seam, a strained rivet, the ordinary damage of taking the ground: my pumps would have held that and given the ship hours. They did not hold what came in that night. That is the thing I cannot get past.

When she struck I was waked by it and I came below as fast as a man can dress and go down. By the time I reached the engine room the water was already over the plates and rising, and rising faster than I have ever seen water rise in a ship. I got the pumps on her, both of them, every suction I could open, and they made no impression at all. The water came up past the pumps as if they were not turning. I have never in twenty years seen sound pumps so wholly beaten, and there is only one way it can happen: either the water is coming in faster than any leak a grounding makes, or the pumps are drawing on nothing because the suctions are not open to the bilge.

I will tell you what I found at the bilge manifold, and I have thought long about whether to say it, because I cannot prove what it means. The bilge valves on the manifold were not as I would have left them or as a watchkeeper holding the engine room should have them. Some were shut that should have been open to put the pumps on the compartments that were filling. I opened what I could in the time I had, in the dark and the rising water, but it made no difference by then, the water was over everything. I had to get my men up and out. I could not stay to see why the valves were as they were.

And I will say this, which I have turned over many nights since. The water in the engine room was not rising the way water rises through a leak in the hull's shell from grounding. It was rising the way water rises when the sea is let in by a large opening low down and under pressure, a fierce live inrush, not a welling. The main sea inlet, the sea-cock, is just such an opening. I did not see its state with my own eyes, in the dark and the water, I was at the manifold and then at the ladder getting my men up, and I will not swear to what I did not see. But I will say that the behaviour of the water was the behaviour of water coming in by the sea-cock, not by a grounding leak, and that the sea-cock is in the engine room, where Lund had been, and where I am told he stayed when the rest came up.

Asked about Lund: he was engaged by the firm over my head. I would not have chosen him. He was a competent enough engineer but he was the owner's man and he kept his own counsel and I never felt I had the engine room wholly in my own hand while he was in it. On the night, he had the watch when she struck. What he did in the engine room in the minutes after she struck, before I got down, I cannot say of my own knowledge, for I was not yet there. I am told by the firemen that he was below at the after end when they were going up. I found the manifold valves wrong when I reached it. I leave the inquiry to make of that what it will. I make no charge against a dead man. I only say the pumps were sound, the water beat them, the valves were wrong, and the water came in like a sea-cock and not like a grounding, and those four things together I have never been able to reconcile with an honest stranding.

## Deposition of Fireman Patrick Geary, survivor

Taken before the present inquiry.

I am Patrick Geary, fireman, of Liverpool. I was in the stokehold of the Marisol on the night she was lost. I came up in the mate's boat.

When she struck, the engineer of the watch, Mr Lund, was in the engine room. We in the stokehold felt the shock and then the telegraph rang to stop and stand by, and we knocked the fires back. After a little while the water began to come into the stokehold from aft, from the engine-room side, fast, and the order came to get up on deck. We all went for the ladders.

I will say what I saw, and I have said it the same way every time I have been asked. As we were going up, I looked back, and Mr Lund was not coming up. He was going the other way, down and aft, toward the after end of the engine room, low down by the plates, where the big sea valve is and where the bilge gear is. I called to him, I think, or someone did, that the water was coming and to come up, and he did not come. He was working at something down there in the water that was already coming up round his legs. I thought at the time he was trying to do something to stop the water, to shut something off, and I thought him a brave man for staying. I have thought since that I do not know what he was doing, only that he was down at the sea valve and the bilge gear when every other man was getting out, and that he never came up. I was the last but one up the ladder and he was still below when I lost sight of him.

That is all I saw. I am no engineer. I only know where the big sea valve is because we firemen go by it, and that is where he was, low down and aft, in the rising water, when the rest of us were going up.

## Deposition of Miss Hester Cardew, cargo surveyor (par-cardew)

Taken before the present inquiry.

I am Hester Cardew, marine cargo surveyor, of Liverpool, in practice some fifteen years. I was engaged on this occasion not by the owners but by a sub-underwriter upon the cargo, to attend the loading of the Marisol and to tally the goods actually put aboard against the manifest, and to certify the tally. I attended at the north quay through the loading on the 11th and the forenoon of the 12th of November.

My method is to stand by the hatch and tally every lift and every parcel as it goes below, lighter by lighter and sling by sling, and to enter it against the manifest, and to note any item on the manifest that I do not see go aboard. My certified tally is in the records and I stand by every figure in it.

I will state the substance. The manifest declared, as the principal item of the cargo, a consignment of machine tools, two hundred and forty cases, of a declared value running to many thousands of pounds, for stowage in numbers two and three holds. I attended the loading of those holds. The cork, the tin plate, the sundries, and the empties I tallied aboard and they answer to the manifest. The machine tools I did not tally aboard. Not one case of the two hundred and forty came over the side or down the hatch while I attended, which was throughout the working of those holds. I was told by the firm's quay agent that the tool cases were to be struck in from the offshore side by the firm's own lighters, away from my station at the quayside hatch, and would not pass my tally. I asked to attend the offshore side. I was told it was not necessary and that the firm would certify that parcel itself. I noted in my tally, against the machine tools, that the item was not seen by me to be loaded and that I was directed away from the loading of it.

I am asked whether I can say the machine tools were not aboard. I can say this: that two hundred and forty cases of machine tools were not loaded at the quay hatch under my tally, where every other item of the cargo was loaded; that I was kept from the only place they could otherwise have been loaded; that a parcel of that weight, had it gone into numbers two and three, would have put the ship down by the stern and not by the head, whereas I saw her trimmed on departure with after ballast, which is not the trim of a ship laden with that weight aft. Whether some lesser thing was put into those holds in place of the tools I cannot say, for I was kept from it. I can say that the declared cargo, the machine tools that gave the manifest its value, did not pass over the side in my sight, and that I was prevented from verifying that they passed over it at all.

That is my evidence. I make no charge. I certify a tally. The tally is what it is.

## Deposition of Mr Mercer, underwriter's agent (par-mercer)

Taken before the present inquiry.

I am Mr Mercer, insurance broker, of the Liverpool market, acting in this matter for the underwriters who carried the risks on the Marisol and her cargo. I placed those risks, dealing with the firm of Veil and Penhallow, and I hold the policy schedules, which are in the records.

I will state the figures, for they are the substance of my evidence. The hull and machinery of the Marisol were insured for a sum I will give in the schedule. She was a steamer of twenty-six years, of a class for which the market in 1924 was very poor, and her sound market value, and still more her broken-up value, were a fraction of the sum insured upon her. I placed the hull cover at the firm's instruction and at the firm's valuation. It is a high valuation for such a ship. Underwriters do not always inquire closely into a hull valuation on an old ship, the premium being the higher for it, and I did not inquire as closely as, knowing what I now know, I wish I had.

The cargo cover is the matter to which I would draw the inquiry's particular attention. The cargo was insured by a declaration under an open policy, the value declared by the firm. The declaration for this voyage was a high one, the bulk of it being the consignment of machine tools, and I will say that the cargo cover was increased, by a further declaration, only a few days before the ship sailed, at the firm's instance, bringing the declared cargo value to its full figure. I thought nothing of it at the time; a shipper increases his declaration when his cargo is made up, and there is nothing in it of itself. I set it beside the surveyor's tally now only because the inquiry has put the two before me together, and I observe, as the inquiry will observe, that the cargo whose value was declared up to the hilt a few days before sailing is the same cargo the surveyor did not see loaded.

I make no charge. I place risks and I keep schedules. I give the inquiry the figures and the dates. The schedules are in the records and speak for themselves.

## Deposition of Mr Marcus Veil, managing owner (par-veil)

Taken before the preliminary inquiry, briefly, and declined to the present inquiry beyond his written answers.

I am Marcus Veil, of the firm of Veil and Penhallow, managing owners of the Marisol. I was in Liverpool throughout the voyage. I had no part in the navigation of the ship and can say nothing of the circumstances of the loss beyond what my master and officers have deposed, in whom I have entire confidence.

The Marisol was a sound ship, well found and well manned. Her cargo was a general one, of which the principal item was a consignment of machine tools, declared and insured at its proper value. The insurances on hull and cargo were placed in the ordinary way through Mr Mercer. I valued the ship at what I held her to be worth to me as a going concern in my trade, which is a higher thing than her break-up price, and I am advised that an owner is entitled to insure his ship at her value to him.

The loss is a grievous one to me. I have lost a ship I valued and five men I engaged, and I am told the loss was an honest accident of fog and current, for which I hold no man to blame, least of all my master, who did all that a master could. I have nothing to add to the evidence of those who were aboard.

(To the present inquiry, in writing, asked to attend and to produce the firm's correspondence touching the cargo declaration and the insurances:) I have produced such papers as the firm holds and as my advisers consider proper. I decline to attend in person, the matter being, as I am advised, concluded by the preliminary inquiry's finding of accidental loss. I refer the inquiry to my master's evidence and to the underwriters, with whom the settlement of the policies is a matter of course.

## Mr Veil's written answers to the present inquiry's interrogatories

The present inquiry, the owner declining to attend, put written interrogatories to him through his advisers. His answers, so far as he gave them, are reproduced with the questions.

Q. Was the consignment of machine tools, declared on the manifest and insured at £37,500, in fact loaded aboard the Marisol?
A. The firm certified the parcel loaded and stowed, and I have no reason to doubt the firm's certificate. The loading of that parcel was attended to by the firm in the ordinary way.

Q. By whom, on the firm's behalf, was the parcel loaded, and on what lighters, and can the firm produce the lighter-men, the tally of the offshore loading, the charter for the carriage of the tools, the bill of lading, or the name of the Lisbon consignee?
A. These are matters of the firm's ordinary business which my advisers consider it unnecessary to particularise to this inquiry, the loss having been found accidental. The firm's certificate answers for the parcel.

Q. Why was the independent cargo surveyor, Miss Cardew, directed away from the offshore side where the tools were said to be loaded?
A. To avoid duplication of the firm's own certification and the encumbering of the loading, the firm certifying that parcel itself. There was no other reason.

Q. Why was the cargo declaration increased to its full figure two days before sailing?
A. Because the parcel was then made up. A shipper declares his cargo when it is made up. There is nothing in it.

Q. Why was the hull insured at £58,000, when you yourself, in your letter of 9 October, describe the ship as worth on a sale about a third of that sum?
A. An owner is entitled to insure his ship at her value to him as a going concern, which is more than her sale or break-up price. I am so advised, and I so insured her.

Q. Why was the second engineer, Lund, engaged over the chief engineer's written objection, and what were the sums paid to him marked "Marisol — special" in the firm's cash book?
A. The engagement of the firm's officers is the firm's affair and not the chief engineer's. As to the cash book, I decline to particularise the firm's private payments, which are not, my advisers say, a proper subject of this inquiry.

Q. The private letters to Lund of 6 and 10 November, produced from the firm's letter-book, instruct him to see to the main injection and the bilge gear, "low down and aft, and quickly, so that the pumps cannot be got on her," and to burn the letter. What did you require of him by those words?
A. I do not accept the construction the inquiry puts on private correspondence taken out of its setting. I gave my second engineer such instructions as an owner gives touching the readiness of his machinery. I have nothing to add, and I am advised to say no more upon it.

(The inquiry records that the owner, on every question that mattered, either referred to the firm's own certificate, declined to particularise, or disputed the construction of his own words, and produced no charter, no consignee, no freight, no lighter-tally, and no innocent account of the payments to Lund or of the private letters. His answers are set out that the assessor may weigh them; the assessor will form his own view of an owner who, asked whether his most valuable cargo was aboard his ship, can answer only that he certified it himself.)

## Cross-examination of Captain Frane before the present inquiry

The underwriters' counsel, not satisfied with the preliminary inquiry's brief handling of the master, examined Captain Frane more closely. The material exchanges are set out.

Q. Captain, the preliminary inquiry found, in effect, that you stood on too long in fog and put your ship on a shoal that lay off your track. What do you say to that?
A. I say I navigated as a careful man navigates in fog on that coast. I reduced to half at eight. I posted my lookouts. I sounded and got no bottom where I was, which agreed with my being outside the shoal. A master cannot stop dead in the water in the open sea every time fog comes down, or no ship would ever make a passage; he reduces, he sounds, he keeps his lookouts, and he goes on with care. I did those things.

Q. And yet you struck.
A. And yet I struck, where my reckoning did not put me. I can only account for it by the inset current, which on that coast in that weather can set a ship in toward the land a long way in a few hours without her knowing it, the fog preventing any fix. I have known it set two knots and more. I believe it set me in past my reckoning. I do not offer it as an excuse. I offer it as the only account I can honestly give of being where I was.

Q. Let me put the graver charge. You abandoned your ship at twenty to midnight. It is said you abandoned too soon, that she might have been saved, and that five men died for your haste.
A. I have heard it said, and it is said by men who were not aboard and who do not know why she was filling. I will tell you the state of things when I gave that order. The water was over the engine-room plates. My chief engineer, a man of twenty years whom I have never known to exaggerate, had both his pumps on her and they were making no impression at all, and he told me to my face he could not understand it and could not hold her. The ship was settling by the stern. To my eye and to his she was filling past all saving, and filling fast. In that state, a master who keeps his people aboard in fog on a falling tide is not a brave man, he is a man who drowns his crew. I gave the order I was bound to give.

Q. You say you could not understand why she was filling. Can you understand it now?
A. Now I am told things I did not know that night. I am told the sea inlet was found open. I am told the bilge valves were found wired shut. I am told my second engineer was below at those very fittings when the rest of my engine-room people were coming up. If those things are so, then I understand at last why my chief's pumps made no impression, and why a light grounding filled my ship in twenty minutes. But I did not know them that night. I knew only that she was filling past the pumps, for a reason no one below could give me, and that I had a few minutes to get my people away. I abandoned a ship I believed past saving, and I am now told she was past saving, and past it by an act I knew nothing of.

Q. One last matter. The second engineer, Lund, was the owner's man, you have said. Did you have any reason, before this voyage, to mistrust him?
A. I had no reason and no proof. I will say only that he was put aboard over my chief's head, by the firm, and that he kept to himself, and that on the one night it mattered he was below at the sea inlet when he should have been at the ladder with the rest. I make no charge. I was on the bridge. I tell you what I have since been told, and what it makes of the night I leave to you.

## Cross-examination of Chief Engineer Such before the present inquiry

Q. Mr Such, you have said your pumps were sound and ample. How sure are you?
A. As sure as a man can be of his own machinery that he has worked and surveyed. The main pump off the engine and the independent Worthington were both in good order. I had them apart at the last survey and I had worked them on the passage out. Between them they could lift a great deal of water, far more than any leak a grounding of the sort we had would open. I would stake my certificate that sound pumps, with the suctions open to the flooding compartments, would have held that ship for hours and very likely saved her. They did not hold her for twenty minutes. There is only one reason sound pumps do not hold: either the water is coming in by something far bigger than a grounding leak, or the suctions are shut against them. That night, as I now understand it, it was both.

Q. You found the bilge valves, you say, not as you would have left them. Be precise.
A. I reached the manifold in the dark with the water already over my boots and rising. I put my hand to the suction valves to make sure they were open to the compartments that were filling, the engine room and number three. They were not open. Some were shut that should have been open. I tried to open them. I will be honest: in the dark and the rising water and the haste, I did not at the time understand why they would not free, or feel the wire upon them; I knew only that I could not get the pumps to draw on the spaces that were filling, and that I had no more time, and that I had to get my men up. I have since been told the wheels were found wired. I did not feel the wire that night. But I felt that the valves were shut and would not readily come open, and that is consistent with what the diver found.

Q. The water itself. You drew, in your deposition, a distinction between a grounding leak and a sea-cock. Explain it to the inquiry.
A. Water through a grounding leak, a started plate or a sprung seam, wells up. It comes in steadily, under the head of water outside the hole, and it rises as the ship settles, but it does not come with violence, and a sound pump holds it. Water through an open sea inlet low down comes in altogether otherwise: it is a live, fierce inrush, under the full head of the sea, through a bore as big as the valve, and it fills a compartment in minutes and no pump on a ship like ours will hold it. What I saw in that engine room was the second kind. I have seen both in twenty years. I knew that night it was wrong, that it was too fierce and too fast for a grounding, though I had not the time nor the light to find the sea inlet and prove it. I know now what the diver found. It only tells me, in cold print, what the water told me in the dark.

Q. You distrusted the second engineer. Did your distrust go to his competence or to something else?
A. Not to his competence; he was a capable enough engineer. It went to his being the owner's man, put in over my preference, and to his keeping his own counsel, so that I never felt the engine room was wholly mine while he was in it. I make no charge against a dead man. I say only that he had the watch when she struck, that he was below at the sea inlet and the manifold when the firemen came up, that I found those very fittings wrong when I reached them, and that the diver found them wrong on the bottom. I have laid those facts before the inquiry. The inquiry may do with them what I, as an engineer and not a judge, may not.

## Deposition of Mr Albert Tunley, quay agent to the firm

Taken before the present inquiry, under compulsion; the witness reluctant.

I am Albert Tunley, clerk and quay agent in the employ of Veil and Penhallow. I had charge, on the firm's behalf, of the loading of the Marisol at the north quay on the 11th and 12th of November.

Asked about the machine tools: I was instructed by the firm, by Mr Veil's written direction, that the tools parcel was a particular one and was to be handled by the firm itself, struck in from the offshore side by the firm's own lighters, away from the quay hatch, and that the ship's officers and the cargo surveyor were not to trouble themselves with it, the firm certifying that parcel. I carried out my instructions.

Asked whether I saw the tools loaded: I was on the quay side. The offshore working was not under my eye in the ordinary way; the firm sent its own men for it. I cannot say of my own knowledge what went aboard on the offshore side, or whether the lighters that worked there carried machine tools or anything else. I did as I was told. I told the mate the tools were aboard because the firm told me to tell him so. I told the surveyor she need not attend the offshore side because the firm told me to keep her off it. I did not myself see two hundred and forty cases of machine tools go into that ship, and I will not say to this inquiry that I did, whatever I may have said on the quay.

Asked whether he thought it strange: a quay agent does as the firm directs and does not question the firm's cargo. I thought it the firm's business and no affair of mine. I think differently now that I am asked on oath. I carried the firm's word to the mate and to the surveyor, and the firm's word was that the tools were aboard. Whether they were aboard, the firm knows and I do not.

## Deposition of Mr Silas Brandt, the firm's bookkeeper

Taken before the present inquiry, under compulsion.

I am Silas Brandt, bookkeeper to the firm of Veil and Penhallow. I keep the firm's books, the cash book among them.

Asked about the entries marked "Marisol — special": there are two such entries against the name of E. Lund, the second engineer, one before the ship sailed and one entered after the loss as due "on completion." They are in the firm's private cash book, not the wages book. The wages of the crew, including Mr Lund's articles wage, are in the wages book in the ordinary way. These two entries are separate and additional, and together they come to several times Mr Lund's wage for the voyage.

Asked what service the entries were for: there is no service named against them. A cash-book entry ordinarily carries a note of what it is for. These carry only "Marisol — special." I made the entries on Mr Veil's instruction and I asked no questions, it not being my place to question the principal's private payments. I do not know what they were for. I was not told.

Asked who, besides Mr Veil, knew of these payments: no one in the office, so far as I know. They were Mr Veil's private dealing. He gave me the figures and the wording and I entered them. The second entry, "on completion," was given to me to enter after the news of the loss came; I queried, mildly, what completion was meant, the ship being lost; Mr Veil said it was a matter settled between him and the man and I was to enter it and say nothing.

Asked about the firm's circumstances in 1924: the firm's trade was poor and the account under pressure through the year, the old ships not paying. I will say no more of the firm's finances than that they were straitened and that the insurance moneys on the Marisol, when they should come, were looked to. I say that as the man who kept the books, and I say it because I am on oath; I would not say it otherwise.

## Deposition of Fireman William Tasker, survivor

Taken before the present inquiry.

I am William Tasker, fireman, of the Marisol's black squad. I was in the stokehold with Geary on the night and came up in the mate's boat.

I can say the same as Geary, for we were together and saw the same. When the water came into the stokehold from the engine-room side and the word came to get up, we went for the ladders. Mr Lund did not come up with us. I saw him myself, down and aft in the engine room, in the water that was rising round him, at the after end where the big sea valve and the bilge gear are. I thought, like Geary, that he was trying to shut something off and save her, and I thought no more of it then. I have thought since that a man trying to save a ship does not go down into the water at the very fittings by which a ship is sunk and stay there while she fills, but at the time I thought him brave and I said so. He never came up.

Asked whether I could see what he was doing with his hands: no. It was dark and the water was rising and I was getting myself to the ladder. I saw where he was, low down and aft by the sea valve, and that he stayed when we went. I cannot say what his hands were at. I can say where he was, and that he chose to be there, and that it was the one place in the engine room a man would be who meant the ship to fill and not to float.

## Deposition of Able Seaman Joseph Renn, survivor

Taken before the present inquiry.

I am Joseph Renn, able seaman, of the Marisol's deck crew. I was in the master's boat. I can speak to the loading and to the abandonment.

Of the loading: I was one of the hands who rigged the heavy derricks in numbers two and three for the tools, on the firm's order. We rigged them and stood by, and the lighters for the tools did not come to the quay side, and we were stood down from the derricks and told the firm would work that parcel itself from the offshore side. I never saw a case of machine tools come over the side or down the hatch, and I was about the decks the whole loading. The cork I saw, and the tin plate, and the cases of sundries, and the empties. The tools I never saw.

Of the abandonment: I helped clear the boats. The master gave the order in good time for the state she was in; she was going down by the stern fast and the engine room was full. The after boat fouled in the falls and went over wrong and was lost with the men in her, and that was a thing of the launching in the dark and the list, not of the master's order, which was given when there was still time to get the other two away clean. I will not have it said the master killed those men. The ship killed them, going down under us faster than any ship should from the knock she took.

Asked about the second engineer: I did not see him. I was on deck. The firemen who came up said he stopped below. That is all I know of it.

## A note on the depositions

You have now the accounts of the men who were aboard and of the witnesses ashore. The master, the mate, and the chief engineer all believe, and say, that the loss had the appearance of an accident, and so it had, from the bridge and from the boats: a ship in fog, a striking, a filling, a hard abandonment in the dark. That is the night as those who lived through it saw it, and they tell it honestly.

But each of these depositions carries, unflagged and in the middle of much ordinary matter, a particular that the man who gave it did not weigh and the preliminary inquiry did not pursue. Read the mate again on the loading and the trim, the surveyor on the tally, the chief engineer on the pumps and the valves and the manner of the water, the fireman on where the watchkeeper went when the others went up, the broker on when the cover was raised and on what. No one of these men held more than his own piece, and so no one of them drew an inference; each piece, alone, is consistent enough with an accident to be passed over. Whether they remain consistent with an accident when they are laid side by side is not a question the depositions answer. It is the question this inquiry exists to answer, and the laying side by side is your work.
