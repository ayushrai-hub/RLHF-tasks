# The Vessel and the Voyage

This section sets out the particulars of the SS Marisol, the arrangement of her holds and her machinery, and the track she ran from the Mersey to the Carrack Shoal. You cannot follow a single deposition without it. The men speak of the after well-deck, the tunnel, the sea-cock, the bilge suctions, and the fore peak as if you stood in the engine room with them. Read this section until those names are a picture in your mind, and return to it whenever a statement loses you.

## General particulars

The Marisol was an iron-and-steel single-screw cargo steamer, built at Sunderland in 1898 for a trade she had long outlived. Her dimensions and registry stood as follows in the certificate of registry produced to the inquiry:

- Official number and port of registry: registered at Liverpool.
- Gross tonnage: 2,840 tons. Net tonnage: 1,790 tons.
- Length between perpendiculars: 312 feet. Breadth: 41 feet. Depth of hold: 24 feet.
- Propulsion: a single triple-expansion engine of 1,450 indicated horsepower, supplied by two single-ended boilers, driving one screw. Service speed loaded, about nine and a half knots.
- Built 1898; twenty-six years old at the time of the loss. Last special survey two years before the casualty; the load-line certificate then current is in the records (see the records log and the appendices).

She was, in plain terms, an old ship, near the end of her economic life, of a class for which the market in 1924 was poor and the breaker's price not much worse than the trading value. You will want to keep that fact by you when you come to read what she was insured for.

## Ownership and management

The Marisol was owned by a single-ship company, the Marisol Steamship Company Limited, the whole of whose shares were held within the firm of Veil and Penhallow or by Mr Marcus Veil personally. The firm of Veil and Penhallow managed her, manned her, insured her, and declared her cargoes. In practice, and for every purpose that matters to this inquiry, the Marisol was Mr Veil's ship: he was her managing owner, he engaged her officers, he placed her insurances, and he made or approved every declaration as to what she carried. Mr Penhallow, the nominal partner, had been an invalid these three years and took no active part.

This concentration is worth marking. There was no separate owner to check the manager, no independent board to question a valuation, no shareholder to ask why an old ship and an ordinary cargo should be insured for sums that, taken together, came to a great deal more than either could fetch in the market. The man who declared the cargo, the man who placed the policies, and the man who would receive the insurance money upon a total loss were one and the same man, and that man never sailed.

## The arrangement of the holds

The Marisol was a three-island ship of the ordinary pattern, with a raised forecastle, a midship bridge house, and a raised poop. Below the main deck she was divided by watertight bulkheads into the following spaces, running from forward to aft:

1. The fore peak tank, right forward, used for water ballast and trimming.
2. Number one hold, the foremost cargo space, under the forecastle and the fore well-deck.
3. Number two hold, abaft number one and forward of the bridge.
4. The bunker spaces and the boiler room, amidships, under the bridge house.
5. The engine room, abaft the boiler room.
6. The shaft tunnel, running aft from the engine room beneath number three hold to the stern gland.
7. Number three hold, abaft the engine room, under the after well-deck.
8. The after peak tank, right aft.

The cargo, such as it was, was stowed in numbers one, two, and three holds. The machine tools, had they been aboard, would by the manifest have gone into number two and number three, the heavy lifts to be worked by the ship's own derricks. The lighter goods, the cork, the empty tins returning, the sundries, were for number one. You will read in the surveyor's tally and the salvage report a great deal about what was, and was not, in numbers two and three, and you should hold the geography of those two holds clearly: number two forward of the engine room, number three abaft it, the shaft tunnel running through number three along the keel.

## The machinery, the bilges, and the sea-cock

Here you must be patient, because the case will turn in part upon a man's knowledge of these fittings, and you cannot weigh his account without understanding them yourself.

A steamer of this kind keeps the water out of her by her hull, and gets out such water as comes in by her bilge pumps. The bilge of each compartment drains to a suction, a pipe with a strainer at its foot, and the suctions are led to a bilge main that the pumps draw upon. The principal bilge pumps on the Marisol were two: a main pump worked off the engine, and an independent steam pump, a Worthington-pattern direct-acting pump, in the engine room, which could draw on the bilge main and discharge overboard regardless of whether the main engine turned. Between the suctions and the pumps stood the bilge valves, on a manifold in the engine room, by which the engineer selected which compartment to pump and shut off the others.

Now mark this carefully. A ship that merely takes the ground on a shoal does not, of itself, fill. She may start a plate, she may strain a rivet, she may make water at a seam; but she makes it at a rate her pumps are built to hold, and a ship of the Marisol's pumping power could keep down a very great deal of leakage and buy hours, not minutes, for her people. A ship fills fast, fills past her pumps, fills until she founders, only if a large body of water is admitted faster than the pumps can throw it out, or if the pumps do not work.

Two fittings, on this ship, could admit such a body of water by the deliberate act of a man below.

The first is the main sea-cock, sometimes called the main injection or the sea inlet, a large valve low in the engine room through which the sea is admitted to the condenser and the pumps in the ordinary way of working the ship. It is a heavy bronze valve on a spindle, opened and shut by a wheel, and it stands always under the full head of the sea outside. Open it wide with nothing to receive the water, and the sea comes in through a bore as thick as a man's arm, low down, under pressure, and no pump on an old cargo steamer will hold against it. It is the one fitting aboard by which a single man, in a minute, working alone in the engine room, can begin to sink the ship from inside her own hull.

The second is the bilge valve manifold itself. If the bilge valves are shut, or worse, secured shut so that they cannot readily be opened, the pumps draw on nothing and the water that comes in is not got out, however the pumps labour. A man who wished a ship to fill, and to go on filling, would open the sea to her low down and would see to it that the bilge suctions could not be put on her.

These two fittings, the open sea-cock and the secured bilge valves, recur throughout the engineering evidence and the salvage diver's report. Keep their function clear. The whole question of whether the Marisol filled by accident or by design lies in them.

## The track of the voyage

The Marisol cleared the Mersey on the forenoon of 12 November 1924 and ran south down the Irish Sea, through Saint George's Channel, and out into the Atlantic approaches, shaping a course to round Finisterre and pass down the Portuguese coast for the Tagus. The track is unremarkable, the weather of the first two days fair to moderate, and the deck log (see the records log) records nothing out of the way until the evening of the 14th.

On the afternoon and evening of the 14th the ship ran into fog off the Portuguese approaches, thickening through the first watch. The Carrack Shoal lies some miles offshore of the coast there, a bank that dries in part at low water and over which there is foul ground, marked on the charts and given a wide berth by prudent navigation. The ordinary track for Lisbon passes well to seaward of it. How the Marisol came to be upon it, in fog, is the very point the Board of Trade fixed upon when it spoke of negligent navigation, and you will read the master's account of the run-in, and the first mate's, and you must weigh whether the ship was set upon the shoal by error or steered toward it by design.

She struck, by the deck log and the reckoning, at about 23:10 ship's time on the night of the 14th. From the moment of striking to the order to abandon was, on the evidence, a very short time. The boats got away near midnight. The ship was abandoned and went down on the falling tide in the small hours.

## The boats and the abandonment

The Marisol carried three lifeboats under davits, port and starboard amidships and one aft, with the buoyant apparatus required by the regulations of the day. On the night of the loss the starboard midship boat was got away with the master, the first mate, and part of the deck watch; the port midship boat with the chief engineer and others; the after boat was not cleared in time and was lost with the ship, and it is in and about the launching of the boats that the five lives were lost. The order in which the boats were cleared, the time the order to abandon was given, and the state of the ship when it was given, are all in dispute or in shadow, and you will read the depositions on the point with care. Whether the master abandoned too soon, as the rumour runs, or abandoned a ship that was in truth past saving, is a question you cannot answer until you have understood why she was filling as she filled.

## The history and condition of the ship

The Marisol had passed through several hands in her twenty-six years. Built in 1898 for a Cardiff firm in the Mediterranean fruit and general trade, she was sold in 1909, again in 1916, and came to Veil and Penhallow in 1921, by which time she was an old ship bought cheap for a declining trade. She had been a sound enough vessel in her day, but she was of a pattern and a power that the post-war market had left behind: too slow, too small, too costly in coal, for the trades that paid. Ships of her class were going to the breakers through 1923 and 1924 faster than buyers could be found for them as going concerns, and her sound market value had fallen accordingly.

Her last special survey, two years before the loss, found her in fair condition for her age, with certain repairs recommended at the next survey, among them attention to the shell plating and, it is worth noting, to the bilge piping. She was not unseaworthy; she was old. Her freeboard and load-line were properly assigned and her certificates current. None of this bears on the cause of the loss directly, but it bears on two matters the assessor must weigh: what she was truly worth, against what she was insured for; and whether her age can be made to explain a filling that her pumps, sound and ample, should have held. The survey found her piping due for attention, not her hull due to open and sink her in twenty minutes from a light grounding.

## A glossary of the terms the witnesses use

The depositions are full of sea terms. The following short glossary will carry the assessor through them.

- **Sea-cock (main sea inlet, main injection):** the large valve low in the engine room through which the sea is admitted to the condenser and pumps in the working of the ship. Opened wide with nothing to receive the water, it admits the sea under full pressure and will sink the ship. Central to this case.
- **Bilge:** the lowest inner part of the hull, where water collects and is pumped out.
- **Bilge suction / bilge valve / bilge manifold:** the pipe, valve, and valve-array by which each compartment's bilge is put on or shut off the pumps. If shut or secured shut, the pumps cannot drain that compartment. Central to this case.
- **Bilge pump:** the pump that throws bilge water overboard. The Marisol had two: the main pump off the engine, and an independent Worthington steam pump.
- **To strand / to take the ground:** to run aground; to touch or hold on the bottom. A light stranding does slight damage; a heavy one may open the hull.
- **To scuttle:** to sink a ship deliberately by letting the sea into her, as by opening the sea-cock or boring or breaching the hull from within. The opposite of a stranding as a cause: a stranding is something done to the ship by the sea and the ground; a scuttling is something done to the ship by a hand within her.
- **Trim:** the way a ship floats, by the head (down at the bow) or by the stern (down at the stern). A heavy cargo low in the after holds trims a ship by the stern. The Marisol sailed down by the head and was ballasted aft to right her, which is not the trim of a ship laden with heavy cargo aft.
- **Ballast:** weight (here, water in the peak tanks) carried to trim or steady a ship, used when cargo does not fill or balance her.
- **Freeboard / load-line:** the height of the deck above the water; the mark to which a ship may legally be loaded. Set against the observed draught, the load-line tells whether a declared weight of cargo was truly aboard.
- **The watch:** the period of duty; the man on watch has charge for that period. The second engineer had the engine-room watch when she struck.
- **Inset current:** a current setting toward the land, which in fog may carry a ship in past her reckoning without her knowing.

Keep this glossary by you. When the chief engineer says the water came in "like a sea-cock and not like a grounding," he is drawing the very distinction on which the case turns, and the assessor who holds the terms clearly will not miss it.

## A picture to carry

Carry this picture as you read. An old ship, near her end, owned and insured and laden to the declaration of one man ashore. A general cargo whose great value, on the paper, was a consignment of machine tools stowed in her two after holds. A fog. A shoal that lay off the proper track. A striking, and then, far too fast for a mere grounding, a filling that ran past her pumps and put her down. And below, in the engine room, in the minutes after she struck, the sea-cock and the bilge valves, and a man.

Hold the picture. Read on.
