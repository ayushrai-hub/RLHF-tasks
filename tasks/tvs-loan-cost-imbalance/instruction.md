A consumer-finance lender extends unsecured personal loans to cross-sold customers and loses far more by approving someone who then defaults than by declining someone who would have repaid. The data in /app/data/tvs_loan.csv has one row per customer with 31 predictors coded V2 through V31, an identifier V1, and the binary label V32, where 1 marks a defaulter and defaulters are a small minority. The columns cover repayment behaviour on the customer's existing loan such as EMI bounces in V2 through V5 and days-past-due counts in V28 through V30, loan and sanctioned-amount fields, demographics such as gender, employment type, resident type and geographic tier, and a product code. None of the columns reveal the outcome, so the behavioural fields are legitimate predictors. V1 is a unique identifier and must not be used as a feature, and V16 is a date of birth that merely repeats the age in V17. The categorical columns V10, V13, V14, V15 and V31 carry inconsistent spellings and codes beyond the documented set, and the bureau columns V21, V23, V24, V26 and V27 are missing for most rows because the customer has no such loan on file, which is informative rather than random. Reference tables under /app/data describe the columns, their roles, missingness, and category counts; they are descriptive aids only.

Build a classifier that ranks customers by default risk and commits to an approve-or-decline decision. The held-out test set is fixed in /app/data/split.csv, a table with columns row_id and split where split is either train or test; train on the rows marked train and score the rows marked test. The row_id of an instance is its 0-based index in tvs_loan.csv. Apply a class-imbalance handling technique so the minority defaulters are not ignored, encode the categorical columns so that inconsistent spellings and categories unseen in training still yield a valid prediction, and handle the informative missingness rather than dropping those columns.

The cost rule is fixed and asymmetric: a false negative, approving a customer who defaults, costs 20, and a false positive, declining a customer who would have repaid, costs 1, while correct decisions cost nothing. Sweep candidate probability thresholds, commit to the single threshold that minimises total cost on the test set, and label every test customer at that threshold as its pred_label. Because false negatives dominate, that cutoff sits well below 0.5, and your chosen-threshold cost must come in below both the 0.5-threshold cost and the cost of approving everyone.

Write metrics.json, predictions.csv, and cost_report.json to /app/outputs/. Read every file under /app/contracts before you design your output, then match the pinned keys, columns, types, sort order, and rounding exactly, because the names are read literally and an invented field fails. The pr_auc field is the average precision, recomputed from your submitted probabilities, so a trapezoidal precision-recall area agrees closely enough. Your held-out ROC-AUC and PR-AUC are scored against a reference model fitted on the same split, so a model that genuinely discriminates defaulters clears the bar while a near-random one does not, and minority-class recall must stay above a floor that a model ignoring defaulters cannot reach. Aim for real discrimination, not constant or degenerate output.

The environment is offline and R is available with the ranger, data.table and jsonlite packages already installed, so do not install packages or reach the network, and do not modify the data files. Create /app/analysis.R and run it with Rscript /app/analysis.R. All outputs must be written to /app/outputs/.
