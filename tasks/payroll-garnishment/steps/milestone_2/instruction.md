Building on /app/pay, add the forward net-pay model, a single-figure net-to-gross gross-up, a per-order garnishment allocation, and a multi-order target-gross solver; all earlier commands keep working. Arithmetic stays in integer cents with no floating point. Where a rounding step is named, round the exact integer quotient to the nearest whole cent with ties going to the even cent (round half to even).

The forward model maps a gross pay in cents to a disposable and a CCPA garnishment pool through three stages, all functions of the gross.

First, progressive tax on the gross. The tax uses fixed marginal brackets, each a threshold in cents and a whole-percent marginal rate that applies to the slice of gross from that threshold up to the next one: 0 percent on the gross from 0 up to 50000, 10 percent on the slice from 50000 up to 200000, 22 percent on the slice from 200000 up to 500000, and 32 percent on every cent of gross at or above 500000. Accumulate each slice's portion times its whole-percent rate as an exact integer in cent-percent units, then divide that total by 100 and round half to even to get the tax in cents. A gross of zero or less owes zero tax.

Second, disposable earnings are the gross minus that tax, floored at zero.

Third, the CCPA garnishment pool on those disposable earnings. The pool is the round-half-to-even of disposable times 25 over 100 and the amount disposable exceeds thirty times the 725-cent federal minimum hourly wage, namely the larger of disposable minus 21750 and zero, taken as the smaller of those two; that smaller value is then capped at an absolute ceiling of 200000 cents. When disposable is 21750 cents or less the pool is zero.

For a single employee that whole pool is what the law lets you garnish, but the pool does not just vanish from the paycheck. It is allocated across the employee's garnishment orders in priority order, and the take-home net is the disposable minus the part of the pool that the orders actually consume.

`net <gross>` prints a single net figure under a one-pool model where the entire CCPA pool is withheld: disposable minus the pool. `grossup <target>` solves the inverse of that one-pool net by bisection: it prints the smallest whole-cent gross whose one-pool net is at least the target. The net is non-decreasing in the gross because every marginal rate is below 100 percent, so disposable rises with gross and the pool claws back only part of each added cent. Solve by bisection over an integer-cent bracket: start lo at the target and hi at four times the target, and while the net at hi is still below the target double hi, up to a guard of 100 doublings; then bisect the half-open interval, taking mid as the floor of lo plus hi over two, setting hi to mid when the net at mid is at least the target else lo to mid plus one, for at most 200 iterations, and the converged lo is the answer. A target of zero prints 0. A missing, negative, or fractional gross or target prints bad_input (exit non-zero).

`target-gross <employee> <target-net>` is the binding solve. The employer wants a chosen take-home net for an employee who carries several garnishment orders, and the unknown is the gross. The orders are allocated in ascending priority then ascending id, senior first, and each order is capped two ways at once. Its stored cap is an absolute ceiling in cents. It also has a fractional ceiling that is a whole percent of the disposable earnings, fixed by the order kind: child-support is 12 percent, tax-levy is 25 percent, creditor is 10 percent, and any other kind is 100 percent. The effective cap for an order at a given disposable is the smaller of its absolute cap and the round-half-to-even of disposable times that kind percent over 100. Because the fractional ceiling is a percent of disposable, the cap itself moves with the gross.

Allocate like this at a candidate gross: compute tax, disposable, then the CCPA pool exactly as above, and walk the orders in ascending priority then ascending id with a running remaining pool that starts at the full pool. Orders that share a priority are not funded one at a time; they form a group that is funded together. For a group, first take every member's effective cap at this disposable and sum them. When the remaining pool covers that group sum, every order in the group is funded to its own effective cap and the remaining pool drops by the group sum before the next, junior group is considered. When the remaining pool is smaller than the group sum the pool binds inside the group and is split across its members in proportion to their effective caps: give each order the integer floor of remaining-pool times its effective cap divided by the group cap sum, which leaves a few leftover cents, then hand those leftover cents out one apiece to the orders with the largest division remainders, breaking ties toward the smaller order id, so the per-order amounts are whole cents that sum to exactly the remaining pool. A binding group consumes the entire remaining pool, so nothing cascades to any junior group. The total allocated is the sum of every order's amount. Because the orders are capped the total allocated can be strictly less than the pool, so part of the pool is never withheld. The multi-order net is the disposable minus that total allocated, and it too is non-decreasing in the gross.

`allocate <employee> <gross>` prints that per-order breakdown at an explicit gross. It computes tax, disposable, and the CCPA pool for the gross, runs the allocation just described, and prints "<order_id> <kind> <amount>" per order in allocation order, ascending priority then ascending id, where amount is the whole-cent figure withheld for that order. An order in a binding group reflects its proportional largest-remainder share, an order in a fully funded group reflects its own effective cap, and an order in a group reached after the pool is exhausted reflects zero. An employee with no orders prints nothing and exits zero. An unknown employee prints not_found (exit non-zero); a missing, negative, or fractional gross prints bad_input (exit non-zero).

`target-gross` prints the smallest whole-cent gross whose multi-order net is at least the target-net, found by the same bisection bracket and bounds as grossup, except the net at each candidate gross is the multi-order allocation net for this employee's orders rather than the one-pool net. The printed gross g satisfies multi-order-net of g at least the target while multi-order-net of g minus one is below it, which is the integer-cent root of the coupled model. A target-net of zero prints 0. An unknown employee prints not_found (exit non-zero); a missing, negative, or fractional target-net prints bad_input (exit non-zero). An employee with no orders garnishes nothing, so its multi-order net equals its disposable and the solve still returns the smallest gross whose disposable clears the target.

The coupling is what makes this resist a shortcut. A solver that reuses the one-pool grossup, withholding the entire CCPA pool, over-withholds whenever the capped orders cannot absorb the whole pool, and lands on a larger gross than the coupled bisection. A solver that freezes each order's fractional cap at the cap it would have at the target disposable, instead of recomputing it at every candidate gross, mis-sizes the senior order and lands on a different gross again. Both naive forms disagree with the nested bisection, which recomputes the pool and every per-order cap at each candidate gross and then allocates in priority order. The target-gross command must return the coupled bisection root, not either shortcut.

`project <employee> --gross <cents> --periods <n> [--exempt <k>]` simulates that many consecutive pay periods at a fixed gross and shows how a per-order arrears balance builds when the pool cannot keep up. The protected floor is no longer the flat 21750: each claimed exemption raises it by 4250, so the floor is 21750 plus 4250 times the exemption count, exempt defaulting to zero, and the pool is recomputed against that adjusted floor exactly as the CCPA pool is otherwise formed. Disposable and the pool are fixed by the gross and hold across every period. Each period every order claims its effective cap at this disposable plus whatever arrears it carries in, and that combined claim drives the same ascending priority then id grouped allocation, a binding equal-priority group split pro-rata by largest remainder with ties to the smaller id. Whatever an order claims but does not receive becomes its arrears, which then compounds by 125 basis points, rounded half to even, before the next period opens, so order and history both bear on the result. After the last period it prints "<order_id> <kind> <total_withheld> <final_arrears>" per order in allocation order, total_withheld the cents collected across all periods and final_arrears the balance still owed. An employee with no orders prints nothing and exits zero. An unknown employee prints not_found, and a missing or non-positive gross or periods or a negative or fractional exemption count prints bad_input, each at a non-zero exit.

`stats <employee>` reports four summary figures over the employee's order caps as "<count> <p75> <variance> <mean>". count is the number of orders. p75 is the seventy-fifth percentile of the caps by the nearest-rank rule: sort the caps ascending, take the rank as the ceiling of 75 times count divided by 100 used as a 1-based index, and read that cap, so the figure is always one of the stored caps and never an interpolation between two of them. variance is the population variance of the caps, count times the sum of the squared caps minus the squared sum, all over count squared, rounded half to even, dividing by count rather than count minus one. mean is the sum of the caps over count rounded half to even, so an average that lands exactly on a half cent goes to the even cent. An employee with no orders prints "0 0 0 0" and exits zero, and an unknown employee prints not_found at a non-zero exit.
